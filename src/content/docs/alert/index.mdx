## Alerts are hypotheses, not conclusions

A security <DefineTerm term="alert">An automated notification produced when detection logic decides that an event might be malicious, anomalous, or in violation of policy. Distinct from the event itself, which is the underlying action: a process executed, a login happened, a file changed.</DefineTerm> is an automated notification produced by detection logic that flagged an event as potentially malicious, anomalous, or in violation of policy. Two parts of that definition carry the analytical weight.

The first is that the notification is **automated**. A person did not decide this event was worth your attention. A rule, a model, or a heuristic did. The call is only as good as the logic behind it, and two engines can produce alerts with identical names for very different reasons. The <DefineTerm term="Detection Mechanism" display="detection mechanism" /> that fired matters as much as the alert it produced.

The second is the word **potentially**. The <DefineTerm term="Detection Logic" display="detection logic" /> flagged activity that could be malicious, but it has not decided whether the activity actually is malicious. That decision is the analyst's. The alert is the first piece of evidence in an investigation, not a verdict on it.

<Callout variant="info" title="Investigative principle">
The investigative value of an alert is inseparable from the detection logic that produced it. Two alerts with identical names from different engines are not the same alert. Knowing *how* the engine decided is the prerequisite to deciding what to do with the result.
</Callout>

### How adversaries exploit detection and alert handling

Skilled adversaries do not only avoid detection; they also *exploit how analysts respond to it*. Eight illustrative patterns follow. Two make the engine's output ambiguous (living off the land, process hollowing), two exploit the people reading it (timing, alert fatigue), two look like normal work (identity abuse, remote-management tools), and two remove the evidence the engine needs (EDR tampering, sensor blinding). Select any card to expand it and watch a short animation of what the pattern looks like in practice.

In current intrusions the most common version is not technical at all: valid credentials and sanctioned tools that look like normal work. And the quietest evasion is no alert at all. A host that stops reporting, an audit policy that changes, or an EDR agent that goes offline is itself an alert worth triaging (ATT&CK v19 calls this tactic <DefineTerm term="Defense Impairment" />, TA0112). When the queue suddenly floods with low-fidelity alerts, sample it and ask whether the volume is a screen for something quieter before swatting it away.

Understanding these patterns is what separates a checkbox investigation from a real one. For each one, the underlying defense is the same: combine signals from multiple detection families, validate against context, and never trust a single surface check on its own.

## Alert in five moves

<Callout variant="info" title="The procedure, before the depth">
1. **Name the mechanism(s) and read the rule.** Which detection families produced the alert, and what did the logic actually look for?
2. **Check the rule's local track record.** Version, last review, local precision, known misses, active exceptions.
3. **Run the four validation checks, each with a stopping point.** Deconflict first (is this authorized testing or a ticketed change?), then gather the evidence with one baseline lookup, one pivot on the entity's recent alerts, and one criticality lookup.
4. **Parse the fields the next phases need.** Entities, times in UTC, process lineage, destinations, identities.
5. **Record a disposition or hand off leads.** A documented Level 0 close is an early Escalation decision and records both verdict axes; anything else hands the next phases a validated alert and a lead list.

How deep to go is set by the [fast-path depth ladder](/docs/fast-path/), and the [Alert report template](/templates/alert-report.md) records what each move found. Not every case starts from a rule: user-reported phishing, external notifications, hunt findings, and provider escalations arrive with no detection logic to read. For those, ask "what was reported, by whom, and what can we verify independently?" in place of "what fired?", then run the same moves. [Other intake](/docs/alert/intake/) covers each channel.
</Callout>

## Example: the privilege escalation alert

A single alert can carry very different meanings depending on context. The example below is a kind most analysts will see eventually, and the same alert can resolve to one of three very different situations. The job of the Alert phase is to gather enough information to know which.

<CaseStudy
  title="“Account granted administrative privileges”"
  scenario="A SIEM rule fires: a user account was granted membership in a privileged group, and the change happened outside an approved change window."
>

The alert is asking a simple question: was this an authorized change, or is it a problem? In practice the same alert can resolve to one of three situations, and the analyst's job is to figure out which. Select each possibility to see what to check.

<Reveal summary="Possibility 1 · Policy violation" variant="example">
A human granted the privileges without going through the approval workflow. This is common, usually mundane, and still worth investigating because it represents a real gap in the change-management process. The analyst confirms it by looking for a change-management ticket, identifying the approver chain, comparing the timestamp against the approval window, and confirming whether the granting account was acting on documented authority. A variant: an engineer used the break-glass (emergency access) account during a declared emergency. Confirm the emergency declaration and the vault checkout record; a break-glass login with neither is not a policy gap but a lead for Possibility 3.
</Reveal>

<Reveal summary="Possibility 2 · Legitimate automation" variant="example">
A deployment script or scheduled task performed the change. This is the easiest possibility to misclassify if the analyst is not familiar with the organization's infrastructure. The analyst confirms it by checking whether the granting account is a known service account, whether the change matches a recurring automation pattern, whether recent CI/CD activity correlates with the timing, and whether the destination group is one that automation is permitted to modify.
</Reveal>

<Reveal summary="Possibility 3 · Adversarial technique" variant="example">
An attacker is using token impersonation, stolen credentials, or another post-compromise method to elevate access. This is the dangerous possibility, and recognizing it quickly is the entire point of triage. The analyst confirms it by reviewing the granting account's recent authentication history for anomalies, looking for lateral movement signals on the source host, checking whether the target account is unusually <DefineTerm term="high value">An account or system that, if compromised, gives the adversary outsized impact: domain administrators, service accounts with broad access, finance approvers, executives, anyone holding the keys to sensitive systems or data.</DefineTerm> or freshly created (newly created admin accounts are a classic persistence pattern), and correlating the host's activity with the broader environment.
</Reveal>

The three sub-pages of the Alert chapter (Detection mechanisms, Validation, <DefineTerm term="Parsing" />) each contribute to disambiguating which of these three situations is in front of the analyst. The detection mechanism explains what the engine saw and how confident it was. Validation tests the signal against the environment, history, and threat landscape. Parsing extracts the specific metadata (which account, which group, which approver, which time) needed to make the call.

</CaseStudy>

<Callout variant="warn" title="Don't skip this step">
A common way investigations fail is not at Uncover but at Alert. Analysts move on to <DefineTerm term="Entity" display="entity" /> work, scoping, or evidence gathering before they understand what the alert was claiming. By the time the gap shows up, you are three phases deep with no anchor.
</Callout>

## Why comprehensive Alert analysis matters

<div class="strategic-grid">
  <div class="strategic-card">
    <h3>Precision classification</h3>
    <p>Separates legitimate operations, policy violations, and adversary activity. The aim: fewer escalations of benign activity, and a better-targeted response to the rest.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="strategic-card">
    <h3>Cleaner scope</h3>
    <p>Knowing the detection mechanism means you know what telemetry the engine saw, and what it did not. That shapes the next phases.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="strategic-card">
    <h3>Benign-close triage</h3>
    <p>Catch benign activity early, whether the rule correctly flagged authorized activity (a benign true positive) or the logic misfired (a false positive). Feed the reasoning back into detection logic so future alerts get sharper.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<KeyTakeaway>
The Alert phase is not about proving what happened. It is about getting clear on *what the alert is claiming* and whether the telemetry supports that claim. Sometimes that alone closes a documented benign pattern (see the [fast path](/docs/fast-path/)). Otherwise it hands the next phases (Subject, Scope, Uncover, Risk) a validated starting point instead of a cold read.
</KeyTakeaway>
