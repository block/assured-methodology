A single <DefineTerm term="PowerShell" /> process may look legitimate. A burst of HTTPS <DefineTerm term="Traffic" display="traffic" /> may look harmless. Together, they reveal that the PowerShell process is <DefineTerm term="Beaconing" display="beaconing" /> to a newly registered <DefineTerm term="Domain" display="domain" />. The picture changes.

## Two perspectives, one picture

<div class="perspective-grid">
  <div class="perspective-card perspective-card--host">
    <div class="perspective-card__label">Host perspective</div>
    <h3>Who initiated the connection?</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Which process opened the socket</li>
      <li>Under which user context it executed</li>
      <li>The command-line arguments that produced it</li>
      <li>What the process did locally before and after</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="perspective-card perspective-card--net">
    <div class="perspective-card__label">Network perspective</div>
    <h3>Where does the traffic go?</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Destination IP, port, and ASN</li>
      <li>Frequency and cadence of communication</li>
      <li>TLS certificate properties and cipher choice</li>
      <li>How the destination compares to historical traffic</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<CaseStudy
  title="A beacon hiding in HTTPS"
  scenario="A workstation initiates outbound HTTPS connections from PowerShell at 03:15 local time."
>

Correlation reveals three signals that, in isolation, are not decisive:

1. The PowerShell process launched outside the <DefineTerm term="User" display="user" />'s normal working hours.
2. Within seconds, the host established encrypted HTTPS sessions to a domain registered 48 hours earlier.
3. The destination has low prevalence in historical DNS data and an untrusted TLS certificate.

Together they describe initial compromise and command-and-control. The correlation is the alert.

<Reveal summary="Why no single signal was decisive">
- PowerShell at 03:00 could be a developer working late.
- HTTPS to a new domain could be a new SaaS tool.
- Low-prevalence destinations exist legitimately.

Each indicator on its own would be tuned out as noise. The combination crosses the threshold.
</Reveal>

</CaseStudy>

## Telemetry to analyze

Four primary surfaces, each with its own signal patterns:

<div class="telemetry-grid">
  <div class="telemetry-card">
    <div class="telemetry-card__icon">🔎</div>
    <h4>DNS queries</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Algorithmically generated <DefineTerm term="DGA">Domain Generation Algorithm. A technique where malware computes domain names at runtime so command-and-control servers can rotate quickly to avoid blocklists.</DefineTerm> domains detected via entropy scoring</li>
      <li>Randomized subdomains</li>
      <li>Extremely short TTLs or fast-flux patterns</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="telemetry-card">
    <div class="telemetry-card__icon">🔗</div>
    <h4>Network connections</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Traffic to recently observed external destinations</li>
      <li>Known malicious ranges from threat intelligence</li>
      <li>Unusual port usage, uncommon protocols</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="telemetry-card">
    <div class="telemetry-card__icon">📊</div>
    <h4>Data transfer patterns</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Automated beaconing (consistent packet sizes at consistent intervals)</li>
      <li>Human-driven traffic is irregular and variable</li>
      <li>Sustained large outbound transfers off-hours</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="telemetry-card">
    <div class="telemetry-card__icon">🛡️</div>
    <h4>Protocol usage</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Tunneling or obfuscation (DNS over HTTPS bypassing inspection)</li>
      <li>TLS abuse (malformed certificates, unusual ciphers)</li>
      <li>Custom encryption layers</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

A finance workstation that normally talks only to internal ERP systems and then initiates TLS sessions to a low-reputation cloud storage provider outside business hours is a clear data-<DefineTerm term="Exfiltration" display="exfiltration" /> signal.

## Correlation strategies

Three dimensions to weigh on every connection:

<div class="strategy-list">
  <div class="strategy-item">
    <span class="strategy-item__num">01</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Connection metadata</h4>
      <p>Timestamps, source and destination IPs, ports, session durations, TLS cipher and handshake behavior.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="strategy-item">
    <span class="strategy-item__num">02</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Payload characteristics</h4>
      <p>Fixed packet sizes, repetitive transmission intervals, hallmarks of automated beaconing.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="strategy-item">
    <span class="strategy-item__num">03</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Destination reputation</h4>
      <p>Domain age, threat intelligence overlap, geographic anomalies, certificate provenance.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

A PowerShell process repeatedly reaching out via HTTPS to a newly registered domain using a self-signed TLS certificate carrying a generic common name is a classic obfuscated-C2 pattern. Without combining process-level context, network <DefineTerm term="Metadata" display="metadata" />, and reputation scoring, the same activity might pass for ordinary HTTPS traffic.

## Command-and-control indicators

Adversaries hide C2 inside legitimate protocols (HTTPS, DNS). The automation underneath leaves fingerprints.

<div class="c2-grid">
  <div class="c2-card">
    <h4>⏱️ Timing patterns</h4>
    <p>Frameworks often default to precise beacon intervals. Legitimate traffic rarely has that cadence.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="c2-card">
    <h4>🔐 TLS certificates</h4>
    <p>Mismatched, self-signed, or generic-CN certificates (impersonated vendor names). Rotating cert chains tied to short-lived infrastructure.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="c2-card">
    <h4>🔎 DNS patterns</h4>
    <p>High-volume queries for random-looking domains (DGA). Rapid IP rotation tied to a single domain (fast-flux). Domains registered in the last 30 days.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="c2-card">
    <h4>📤 Exfiltration indicators</h4>
    <p>Large compressed transfers outside normal hours. Outbound spikes to low-reputation destinations.</p>
  </div>
</div>

An internal database <DefineTerm term="Server" display="server" /> that suddenly sends gigabytes of zipped data to a generic cloud storage provider at 02:30 is a strong signal of active data theft. Even if the channel is encrypted, the volume, timing, and destination characterize the intent.

<KeyTakeaway>
Network correlation moves you from isolated alerts to unified adversary visibility. Identify the intrusion before <DefineTerm term="Lateral Movement" display="lateral movement" />. Map the chain from initial <DefineTerm term="Execution" display="execution" /> to external communication. <DefineTerm term="Containment" /> becomes a question of when, not if.
</KeyTakeaway>
