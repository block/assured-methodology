<Callout variant="info" title="One phase complete. The next begins.">
Alert told the analyst what fired and gave the signal structured shape. Subject is the next step: who acted, with what credentials, and in what context. Done well, the transition is invisible. The work flows from one phase to the next without the analyst having to re-derive what they already knew.
</Callout>

## What the transition is for

The handoff between phases is where investigations either gather momentum or stall. A clean Alert-to-Subject transition does three things:

  <div class="transition-role">

# Three deliverables from Alert

By the time you leave the Alert phase, you should have:

<div class="deliverables-grid">
  <div class="deliverable-card">
    <h4>🔧 Technical foundation</h4>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Detection mechanism analysis</strong>, what fired, by what logic, with what confidence.</li>
      <li><strong>Structured alert data</strong>, normalized fields: processes, commands, hashes, identifiers.</li>
      <li><strong>Validation results</strong>, the four-dimensional check on signal fidelity.</li>
      <li><strong>Telemetry correlation</strong>, cross-platform context tying the alert to its environment.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="deliverable-card">
    <h4>🧠 Contextual intelligence</h4>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Baseline analysis</strong>, how this activity compares to the entity's history.</li>
      <li><strong>Attack chain reconstruction</strong>, any multi-event correlation that emerged during validation.</li>
      <li><strong>Threat intelligence fusion</strong>, campaign attribution, TTP mapping where applicable.</li>
      <li><strong>Temporal context</strong>, the chronology of what happened, in what order.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <div class="deliverable-card">
    <h4>⚖️ Initial risk framing</h4>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Asset criticality classification</strong>, business value of the systems involved.</li>
      <li><strong>Regulatory mapping</strong>, applicable compliance regimes (SOX, HIPAA, GDPR, PCI-DSS).</li>
      <li><strong>Business impact framing</strong>, first-pass estimate of operational, financial, reputational consequence.</li>
      <li><strong>Escalation criteria</strong>, thresholds defined; Subject and later phases test them.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

# Preparing for Subject analysis

Alert told you *what fired*. Subject asks *who acted*. To make that question answerable, organize three things before you start.

## Entity identification

From the parsed Alert data, list every entity that touched the activity:

- **Primary entities**: users, systems, services directly named in the detection.
- **Secondary entities**: related accounts, connected systems, dependent services that may have been affected.
- **High-value targets**: critical assets or privileged accounts that could become targets for lateral movement.

## Context requirements

The Subject phase needs organizational context the Alert phase didn't have to assemble:

- **User role information**: job functions, department, business responsibilities.
- **System classifications**: criticality ratings, data sensitivity, operational importance.
- **Relationship mappings**: network connectivity, access dependencies, trust relationships.

## Investigation parameters

Set the shape of the Subject-phase work before you start:

- **Analysis depth**: how much behavioral, authorization, authentication, and relationship analysis is the alert worth?
- **Time boundaries**: historical window for baseline comparison and pattern detection.
- **Escalation thresholds**: what would the Subject phase need to find for you to skip ahead and escalate?

<Callout variant="info" title="The handoff is the artifact">
If a peer picked up this alert tomorrow, would the Alert-phase work give them a clean starting point? That's the test. The transition isn't ceremony, it's quality assurance for everything downstream.
</Callout>

<KeyTakeaway>
A clean Alert phase ends with a structured, validated alert + an entity list + a context plan. The Subject phase doesn't re-derive that work. It builds on it.
</KeyTakeaway>

<ChapterExample
  title="A Power-Empire intrusion, step by step"
  scenario="A finance team analyst receives an EDR alert flagging suspicious PowerShell activity on a workstation. The chapter so far has covered detection mechanisms, validation, and parsing. This walkthrough is what the whole chapter looks like applied to one real-shaped incident."
>

<ExampleStepper steps={["The alert fires", "What family fired", "Validate the signal", "Parse the metadata", "Decide what is next"]}>

  <div data-step class="example-step">
    <h3>Step 1. The alert fires</h3>
    <p>At 14:18 local time, the EDR console produces an alert on a finance team workstation. The first sight an analyst gets is something like this:</p>

```
Alert: Suspicious PowerShell activity
Severity: High
Confidence: 72%
Host: laptop-finance-09
User: dlin (finance.analyst)
Process: powershell.exe (PID 8432)
Parent: outlook.exe (PID 6210)
Command line: powershell.exe -NoP -W Hidden -EncodedCommand JABzAGEAcgB...
Network: TLS connection to mail-store-09.invoice-records[.]com:443
First seen: domain registered 4 days ago
```

    <p>Before anything else, the analyst reads the alert calmly. The fields that immediately matter for the next steps are the <strong>parent process</strong> (Outlook), the <strong>command-line flags</strong> (<code>-NoP -W Hidden -EncodedCommand</code>), and the <strong>network destination</strong> (an unfamiliar domain registered four days ago). All three are consistent with an initial-access attempt using a tool like <DefineTerm term="PowerShell Empire">An open-source post-exploitation framework that uses PowerShell agents on Windows and Python agents on Linux. Empire was widely used by red teams and continues to influence threat-actor tradecraft. Many real-world intrusions still show Empire-style command-line and network patterns.</DefineTerm> or a similar PowerShell-based agent. None of those signals alone is decisive. The combination is what raises the priority.</p>

    <p class="example-step__what-not"><strong>What an analyst could miss here:</strong> reading "Severity: High" and acting on it before noticing that the confidence is only 72% and the parent process is Outlook. Severity alone does not tell the analyst whether to treat this as a verdict or a hypothesis.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-step class="example-step">
    <h3>Step 2. What family fired</h3>
    <p>The alert was produced by a behavioral engine, not a signature match. The 72% confidence and the description (<em>"Suspicious PowerShell activity"</em>) make that clear. Behavioral engines fire on patterns, not on known-bad hashes, so the analyst weighs the alert accordingly.</p>

    <Reveal summary="Why this matters for what comes next" variant="example">
      <p>If a signature engine had fired instead, the analyst would be looking at a hash that matches a known malware family, and the immediate next step would be containment plus campaign-scope search. Because this is behavioral, the next step is corroboration. The engine has flagged a pattern. The analyst's job is to confirm the pattern reflects intent.</p>
      <p>This is exactly the distinction the Detection Mechanisms chapter set up. The detection family changes the investigative posture.</p>
    </Reveal>
  </div>

  <div data-step class="example-step">
    <h3>Step 3. Validate the signal</h3>
    <p>The analyst runs the validation framework against the alert before committing more time. Four dimensions, three strategies.</p>

    <ul class="example-step__list">
      <li><strong>Technical fidelity.</strong> Cross-platform telemetry agrees: the EDR sees the PowerShell invocation, the proxy sees the outbound TLS connection, and the firewall logs the destination IP. The signal is coherent across sources, which is the technical-fidelity check passing.</li>
      <li><strong>Environmental context.</strong> The user (dlin) has a baseline of normal Outlook activity and never invokes PowerShell as a job function. The host is a standard finance laptop, not a developer workstation. The behavior is anomalous for this entity, which is the environmental check failing in a useful way.</li>
      <li><strong>Threat intelligence.</strong> The destination domain (invoice-records[.]com) was registered four days ago, has low DNS prevalence, and matches the naming pattern of a phishing campaign reported by a peer organization last week. Threat intel raises confidence, not lowers it.</li>
      <li><strong>Business impact.</strong> Finance laptops have access to payment and vendor systems. A confirmed compromise here would have direct financial and regulatory consequences. The business dimension says this alert is worth full attention.</li>
    </ul>

    <p class="example-step__what-not"><strong>What an analyst could miss here:</strong> stopping at one dimension. A "the command line looks bad" reaction without the environmental and business checks is a guess, not validation. All four dimensions together give the analyst something defensible.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-step class="example-step">
    <h3>Step 4. Parse the metadata</h3>
    <p>Validation said yes, this is worth investigating. Parsing extracts the specific facts the next phases need.</p>

    <ul class="example-step__list">
      <li><strong>Command line:</strong> the <code>-EncodedCommand</code> blob decodes to a PowerShell <DefineTerm term="download cradle">A short PowerShell or shell snippet that downloads and executes a remote payload in one step. Classic cradles use <code>Invoke-WebRequest</code> or <code>System.Net.WebClient</code> to fetch the next stage straight into memory.</DefineTerm> that pulls a second-stage payload from <code>invoice-records[.]com/upd</code>. The analyst captures the decoded command and the URL.</li>
      <li><strong>Process tree:</strong> <code>outlook.exe → powershell.exe</code>. Outlook spawning PowerShell is the classic phishing-with-macros initial-access pattern. The analyst captures the PID, parent PID, and the user context.</li>
      <li><strong>Network activity:</strong> outbound HTTPS to <code>invoice-records[.]com</code> with a self-signed TLS certificate carrying a generic common name. The analyst captures the destination, the certificate fingerprint, and any beaconing pattern visible in the next 30 minutes.</li>
      <li><strong>File system:</strong> <code>%TEMP%</code> shows a recently dropped <code>upd.ps1</code> file. The analyst captures the file hash, creation time, and the process that wrote it (PowerShell).</li>
      <li><strong>Schema:</strong> the alert and its corroborating events all land in the SIEM normalized to OCSF, so the analyst can issue a single query joining endpoint, network, and identity data for the user and host in the relevant window.</li>
    </ul>

    <p>The output of the parsing step is a structured, queryable record. The next analyst who picks this up sees not the original blob but the extracted facts.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-step class="example-step">
    <h3>Step 5. Decide what is next</h3>
    <p>The Alert phase ends here. The analyst now has enough to hand off to the Subject phase with confidence:</p>

    <ul class="example-step__list">
      <li><strong>Primary entities:</strong> the user dlin, the host laptop-finance-09, the destination domain invoice-records[.]com.</li>
      <li><strong>Secondary entities:</strong> the email sender (assumed to be the phishing originator, to be confirmed in Subject), any other recipients of the same email, the AD service account dlin's laptop authenticated against.</li>
      <li><strong>Investigation parameters:</strong> a 24-hour historical window for baseline comparison, depth of behavioral analysis appropriate for a finance role, escalation thresholds that say "if any sibling host shows the same activity, escalate immediately."</li>
    </ul>

    <p>The analyst is not deciding yet whether this is a true positive or a contained incident. The Alert phase has done its job: produced a structured, validated, parsed record that the next phases (Subject, Scope, Uncover, Risk, Escalation, Documentation) can act on without redoing the work.</p>

    <Reveal summary="What a less careful analyst would have done" variant="example">
      <p>A junior analyst might have done one of three things wrong:</p>
      <ul>
        <li>Closed the alert as "PowerShell, probably scripting" because the user works in finance and "wouldn't be doing anything malicious." This skips validation and trusts the user instead of the evidence.</li>
        <li>Escalated to incident response immediately on "Severity: High" without parsing. The IR team then redoes the parsing work from scratch, wasting time.</li>
        <li>Spent two hours decoding the PowerShell command in detail but never checked whether other recipients got the same email. The campaign continues to land on other users while the analyst goes deep on one host.</li>
      </ul>
      <p>The methodology is what prevents each of those failure modes. Validation catches the dismissal. Parsing structures the handoff. Subject and Scope expand from this host to the campaign.</p>
    </Reveal>
  </div>

</ExampleStepper>

</ChapterExample>
