## Common failure modes

<div class="pit-grid">
  <div class="pit"><h3>Post-hoc rationalization</h3><p>Writing the record after the verdict and, without meaning to, writing the story that justifies it: uncertainty disappears, dead ends vanish, and the reasoning reads cleaner than it ran. The record becomes a defense brief instead of an investigation log. Delayed documentation is the usual cause, and it also costs detail: timelines blur and context goes missing. Fix: capture reasoning at decision time (the timeliness standard exists for this), and keep the dead ends in the record; they are evidence of rigor, not noise.</p></div>
  <div class="pit"><h3>Inconsistent terminology</h3><p>Different analysts using varied terms for the same concepts. Creates confusion and complicates trend analysis. Fix: the team's controlled vocabulary (the <a href="/docs/glossaries/clear/">C.L.E.A.R. glossary</a> is a starting point), MITRE ATT&CK for adversary behavior, VERIS for incident categorization, and the fixed two-axis verdict vocabulary, enforced through templates and review.</p></div>
  <div class="pit"><h3>Missing negative findings</h3><p>Checks that found nothing go unrecorded. Negative results rule out attack vectors, establish the case's boundaries, and stop the next analyst from re-running the same queries. Fix: record each hypothesis tested and disproven, with what was examined, not just "nothing found."</p></div>
  <div class="pit"><h3>Excessive jargon</h3><p>Overuse of technical terminology without explanation limits the value to non-technical stakeholders. Fix: precision plus clear explanations for specialized terms, especially in the decision header's bottom line and the business impact section.</p></div>
  <div class="pit"><h3>Missing context</h3><p>Focusing on technical details without business context or impact significance. Reduces strategic value. Fix: always connect technical findings to business risk, operational impact, and organizational priorities.</p></div>
  <div class="pit"><h3>Incomplete records</h3><p>Fields left blank, sections skipped, references not linked. The record exists but does not support future review. Often caused by writing after the case is closed. Fix: required fields validated before close.</p></div>
  <div class="pit"><h3>Missing evidence chains</h3><p>The record states a conclusion but does not say which evidence supports it. The next reader cannot verify the reasoning without redoing the work. Fix: every claim cites its query, log ID, or artifact hash.</p></div>
  <div class="pit"><h3>Sanitization mistakes</h3><p>Records that copy PII, regulated data, or sensitive operational detail into places that do not need it: the narrative, the ticket title, the shared or training version. The record is complete and is now a data exposure. Blame language about the people involved is the same mistake in another form. Fix: keep raw evidence in the access-controlled evidence store, reference it from the narrative, share only sanitized derivatives, and write about people as the <a href="/docs/documentation/standards/#people">standard on writing about people</a> describes.</p></div>
  <div class="pit"><h3>Form over substance</h3><p>Long documentation that meets the standard but says nothing useful. Fields filled; value absent. The template was treated as the goal. Fix: closure review checks rationale, not field count.</p></div>
</div>

<Reveal summary="Other pitfalls to recognize" variant="warn">

**Ambiguous ownership.** Documentation quality suffers when responsibility is unclear. When it's not clear who should document what aspects of an investigation, critical information may be assumed to be someone else's responsibility and consequently omitted. The fix is clear documentation roles, whether via a dedicated scribe for major incidents (the established IR role whose only job is the record) or explicit assignment during routine event handling.

**Wrong level of detail.** Overly verbose records consume excessive time to create and review. Overly brief records omit critical context. Documentation standards should provide clear guidance on expected depth for different event types and severity levels.

**Accessibility and discoverability gaps.** Well-documented events provide limited value if the information cannot be easily located. Implement structured knowledge repositories with consistent categorization, tagging, and search to ensure documentation remains accessible for reference, training, and improvement.

</Reveal>

## Incomplete records: the recurring failure

The most common failure mode deserves its own treatment. <DefineTerm term="Event Triage" display="Triage" /> urgency is exactly when context goes uncaptured: the key contextual information is the first thing to be skipped or deferred.

<div class="omission-grid">
  <div class="omission-col">
    <h3>Common documentation omissions</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Decision rationale and risk acceptance justifications</li>
      <li>Alternative courses of action considered but rejected</li>
      <li>Stakeholder consultations and approvals obtained</li>
      <li>Technical limitations that constrained response options</li>
      <li>Assumptions made when working with incomplete information</li>
      <li>Temporary workarounds implemented during response</li>
      <li>Failed attempts or approaches before successful resolution</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="omission-col">
    <h3>Consequences of incomplete documentation</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Inability to justify actions during audits or legal proceedings</li>
      <li>Repeated mistakes due to lost lessons from previous incidents</li>
      <li>Inefficient knowledge transfer to new team members</li>
      <li>Difficulty establishing patterns across multiple incidents</li>
      <li>Challenges in demonstrating regulatory compliance</li>
      <li>Inaccurate metrics and effectiveness measurements</li>
      <li>Reduced confidence in security team capabilities</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout variant="warn" title="The structural fix">
Documentation must be explicitly integrated as a fundamental part of triage. Analysts should document key findings, justification for risk ratings, and decisions at defined milestones (at minimum, after initial review, before escalation, and at closure; ideally at every phase transition, as Standards describes), using standardized templates or case-management fields. Five minutes of "this is what I just observed" notes at each milestone produces a better record than two hours of reconstruction at the end. The timeliness policy and automation that enforce this are covered under [Standards](/docs/documentation/standards/#timeliness).
</Callout>

<KeyTakeaway>The pitfalls are predictable. Catching them is mostly a matter of writing during the investigation, keeping evidence chains tight, and treating the form as scaffolding rather than the goal. The methodology's defense against documentation failures is structural: enforced templates, defined milestones, and quality-assurance review on a regular cadence. See **[the five downstream uses of documentation](/docs/documentation/#downstream-uses)** for what well-written records get used for after the case is closed.</KeyTakeaway>
