# Three standards that matter most

<div class="std-grid">
  <div class="std">
    <h4>🧱 Format and structure</h4>
    <p>The same case sections appear in the same order across every record. Summary, timeline, entities, evidence, verdict, actions, open questions. Future readers know where to find each piece without searching.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="std">
    <h4>🔍 Clarity and precision</h4>
    <p>Plain language. Named entities (not "the user," but "alice@example.com"). Specific timestamps in a consistent timezone. Quoted evidence rather than paraphrased. No abbreviations the team has not agreed on.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="std">
    <h4>⏱️ Timeliness</h4>
    <p>Documentation written during or immediately after the investigation captures detail that is gone an hour later. The standard is "live as you go" with cleanup at the end, not "I will document this tomorrow."</p>
  </div>
</div>

# The format the methodology recommends

A case record includes, in order:

1. **Summary.** Two sentences. What happened, what was decided.
2. **Timeline.** Sequential events with timestamps. Adversary actions and analyst actions both.
3. **Entities.** Primary and secondary, with their assessments.
4. **Evidence chain.** Each piece of evidence with its source and any queries used.
5. **Risk verdict.** Impact, likelihood, priority, with rationale.
6. **Actions taken.** What was done, by whom, when, and why.
7. **Open questions.** What was not resolved and what would change the outcome.
8. **References.** Linked artifacts, related cases, applicable playbooks.

<KeyTakeaway>Standards are not paperwork. They are the protocol that makes documentation useful to future readers, including the analyst who wrote it.</KeyTakeaway>
