## Two different jobs

<div class="vs-grid">
  <div class="vs-card vs-card--triage">
    <span class="vs-card__tag">Event triage</span>
    <h3>Framing the puzzle</h3>
    <p>Goal: assemble enough of the picture to answer one question: is this real, and does it need more than triage? The work ends with a verdict and its record: a closure record for most cases, a handoff packet when it escalates.</p>
    <p>Mindset: structured, time-bounded, escalates without hesitation when a criterion is confirmed.</p>
    <p>Output: structured triage record, ATT&CK mapping, recommended priority.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="vs-card vs-card--ir">
    <span class="vs-card__tag">Incident response</span>
    <h3>Completing the puzzle</h3>
    <p>Goal: contain the incident, eradicate the adversary, recover affected systems, and produce the post-incident report. The work ends with the organization returning to a known-good state.</p>
    <p>Mindset: deep, sustained, defaults toward maintaining containment until eradication is confirmed.</p>
    <p>Output: contained incident, restored systems, root cause analysis, lessons learned.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout variant="info" title="Where the line is">
The line falls at the point where investigation transitions to coordinated response. Triage answers "is this real, and does it need more than triage?" IR decides what to do about it. Both phases require investigation, but the questions, the timelines, and the audiences are different. The standards mapping shows <a href="/docs/standards-mapping/#where-the-triage-boundary-sits">how this boundary maps to CSF 2.0 DE.AE-08 and RS.MA-01</a>.
</Callout>

### The vocabulary: event, alert, incident

The three terms get used interchangeably in casual conversation and lose precision under pressure. ASSURED treats them as distinct.

<div class="vocab-grid">
  <div class="vocab-card vocab-card--event">
    <span class="vocab-card__tag">EVENT</span>
    <h4>Any observable occurrence</h4>
    <p>Any observable occurrence involving computing assets, including physical and virtual platforms, networks, services, and cloud environments (NIST SP 800-61r3, 2025): a login, an email delivered, a blocked connection, a configuration change. An event is neither good nor bad on its own. It is raw observability data.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="vocab-card vocab-card--alert">
    <span class="vocab-card__tag">ALERT</span>
    <h4>An event meeting a threshold</h4>
    <p>A particular event, or an aggregated group of events, that crosses a threshold requiring investigation. Many alerts are possible adverse events in NIST's terms: occurrences that may have a negative effect, still to be confirmed. Alerts are the boundary between observability and SOC work.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="vocab-card vocab-card--incident">
    <span class="vocab-card__tag">INCIDENT</span>
    <h4>A violation, actual or imminent, requiring response</h4>
    <p>An occurrence that actually or imminently jeopardizes, without lawful authority, the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of information or an information system, or that violates or imminently threatens to violate law, security policies, security procedures, or acceptable use policies (NIST SP 800-61r3, 2025, adopting the definition in the Federal Information Security Modernization Act, FISMA, of 2014). Typically confirmed out of one or more alerts. Escalation hands IR a case; whether it becomes a declared incident is the call of the role the protocol names (Tier 2 or the IR lead in this methodology's model).</p>
  </div>
</div>

The words carry different obligations: an alert is something triage acts on, an incident is something IR coordinates, and the line between them is where the Escalation decision lives.

## Event triage in depth: framing the puzzle

What triage is, and the seven questions ASSURED asks, is the <a href="/docs/intro/">introduction</a>'s job. Here it matters only where triage ends. In NIST SP 800-61r3 terms, which map incident response onto the functions of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0, triage is the front edge of Detect and Respond: adverse-event analysis up to incident declaration (DE.AE, through DE.AE-08) and the triage and validation of incident reports (RS.MA-02). Its priority and escalation decision are inputs to the categorization, prioritization and escalation that incident management performs on a declared incident (RS.MA-03, RS.MA-04). It puts just enough pieces together, from the most informative, high-confidence elements (corroborated indicators, asset relevance, contextual <DefineTerm term="Telemetry" display="telemetry" />), to answer one question: <strong>is this real, and does it need more than triage?</strong> Four habits keep it that way:

<div class="triage-tenets">
  <div class="triage-tenet">
    <h3>High-confidence starting points</h3>
    <p>Use well-structured data such as known malicious IPs, hashes, or attack techniques as anchors. These serve as the "corner pieces" that guide rapid assessment: high-confidence starting points that tell the analyst where to look first, to be corroborated by behavior and context before they count as proof.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="triage-tenet">
    <h3>Contextual awareness</h3>
    <p>Incorporate asset classification, user roles, geographic origin, and behavioral baselines. Context transforms raw technical data into meaningful security insights.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="triage-tenet">
    <h3>Targeted information gathering</h3>
    <p>Focus on collecting only the data required to make a go / no-go decision. Avoid exhaustive log reviews unless directly relevant to initial indicators. Efficiency prevents analysis paralysis.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="triage-tenet">
    <h3>Defined escalation criteria</h3>
    <p>Build and follow clearly defined rules that dictate when an event should hand off to incident response. Criteria should be threat-informed, asset-aware, and consistently applied.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<Reveal summary="Why rapid closure of non-threats matters" variant="info">

A critical yet often overlooked component of successful triage is the rapid closure of non-threats. When an alert is confirmed as a false positive or as expected, authorized behavior (a benign true positive), it should be quickly documented and closed, with rationale captured for future detection tuning or suppression-rule creation.

This prevents alert-backlog accumulation and keeps analyst attention focused on genuine security concerns. By design, triage prevents analysts from getting bogged down in low-fidelity events, ensuring that time and energy are preserved for high-risk scenarios.

The methodology's operating model for this is the [fast path](/docs/fast-path/): a defensible five-minute close is a Level 0 pattern close (an alert matched against a documented pattern and recorded in five fields), not a shortcut through the phases.

Done right, fast closes are what buy the time for the cases that need the full arc.

</Reveal>

## Incident response in depth: completing the puzzle

Once triage concludes that an event is a true positive and potentially harmful, and the Escalation decision engages IR, the work transitions to <DefineTerm term="Incident Response" display="incident response" />, a thorough and methodical process where analysts seek to complete the entire picture. IR takes the partially assembled puzzle and builds it out in full: root cause, attacker behavior, impact scope, and remediation path.

These workstreams run concurrently, not in order: containment usually starts before reconstruction is complete (NIST SP 800-61r3 maps incident response to concurrent CSF 2.0 functions). Throughout, from the first hours, legal and compliance assess notification obligations (some regulatory clocks run 72 hours or less from awareness), and communications and executive leadership engage as impact becomes clear.

<div class="ir-phases">
  <div class="ir-phase">
    <h3>Full attack reconstruction</h3>
    <p>Identify initial vector. Map all attacker activity. Build a detailed timeline of lateral movement, privilege escalation, data access, or exfiltration.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="ir-phase">
    <h3>Containment and control</h3>
    <p>Implement measures to prevent further damage. Isolate endpoints, revoke credentials, block malicious infrastructure, segment the network. Balance security with business continuity.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="ir-phase">
    <h3>Eradication and remediation</h3>
    <p>Remove all traces of attacker presence. Rebuild systems from clean images, patch exploited vulnerabilities, close configuration gaps, validate that the same vectors cannot be re-exploited.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="ir-phase">
    <h3>Recovery and lessons learned</h3>
    <p>Restore systems and improve defenses. Conduct a formal post-incident review.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Incident response is also more than technical work: structured communication with stakeholders, coordination across teams, and regulatory and contractual obligations, with legal, compliance, executive leadership, and sometimes third parties engaged through the channels the <a href="/docs/escalation/protocols/">protocols</a> name.

<h2 id="pre-authorized">Containment authority: the pre-authorized immediate actions</h2>

"IR owns containment" has one deliberate exception. Mature SOCs maintain a short, written list of <strong>pre-authorized immediate actions</strong> that triage may execute without waiting for IR: typically isolate a host, disable an account, revoke an account's sessions, and disable a consented application's sign-in. The authority comes from the playbook, agreed in advance with IR and the business owners, not from the analyst's judgment in the moment. When the worked example shows the triage analyst disabling an account and isolating hosts before IR arrives, that is this list at work, not triage doing IR's job.

Four rules govern the list:

- **Preserve before you contain.** Capture the volatile evidence and export the records a containment action would destroy or age out (memory, sign-in and session records, the definition of a rule before it is disabled), then act. <a href="/docs/escalation/preserve/">Preserve before you contain</a> gives the order of volatility, the tip-off check, and the minimum custody record.
- **Reversible only.** Everything on the list can be undone (re-enable the account, release the host), and every action is logged in the communication record and reported in the handoff packet's containment section. Actions that cannot be undone, such as a credential reset or removing an application's consent grant, need IR or owner approval unless the protocol lists them explicitly as pre-authorized for that scenario.
- **Verify, do not assume.** After acting, confirm the action took effect (no new sign-ins from the attacker's infrastructure, the host shows contained) and record the verification time in the packet.
- **IR can suspend the list.** If IR is running covert observation of this actor, the list is suspended by IR's call.

Every action also breaks something, for the business or for the investigation. Know the cost before the moment comes:

## How to know which work this is

<div class="which-grid">
  <div class="which-card">
    <p><strong>If the question is "is this real?"</strong></p>
    <p class="which-answer">It is triage.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="which-card">
    <p><strong>If the question is "how do we stop it?"</strong></p>
    <p class="which-answer">It is incident response.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="which-card">
    <p><strong>If the answer to "is this real?" is yes (or the impact is too high to wait for it) and nobody owns "how do we stop it?" yet</strong></p>
    <p class="which-answer">It is in the handoff. Escalation is the bridge.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<KeyTakeaway>Triage frames the puzzle: just enough pieces to recognize what you are looking at and whether it deserves further response. IR completes the puzzle: full reconstruction, containment, eradication, recovery. The methodology treats them as related but separate. The Escalation decision is the deliberate transition between them, or the documented close that makes one unnecessary.</KeyTakeaway>
