<div class="intro-lede">
  <div class="intro-lede__primary">
    A security alert is a question the detection engine is asking the analyst. <em>Is this real, is it malicious, how serious is it, and what should happen next?</em>
  </div>

  <div class="intro-lede__secondary">
    Event triage is the work of answering those questions with confidence, and it is a distinct skill from broader incident response. Three words matter from here on: an <strong>event</strong> is any observable occurrence, an <strong>alert</strong> is an event a detection flagged for a human, and an <strong>incident</strong> is a confirmed (or imminent) violation that incident response (IR) owns. Triage turns alerts into one of two outcomes: a documented close or a declared incident. ASSURED is a structured, repeatable methodology for doing that work consistently. Its seven letters, <strong>A</strong>lert, <strong>S</strong>ubject, <strong>S</strong>cope, <strong>U</strong>ncover, <strong>R</strong>isk, <strong>E</strong>scalation decision and <strong>D</strong>ocumentation, cover the full arc from the moment an alert fires to the moment the analyst closes the case or hands it off.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="intro-section-head">
  <span class="section-overline">The method in one screen</span>

## ASSURED in seven questions

</div>

The letters are the method. Each one is a question the analyst answers before moving on:

<SevenQuestions label="ASSURED in seven questions" />

Two naming traps are worth defusing now. The two S's: **who before where**. Subject resolves the person or system behind the activity; Scope then draws the lines around it. You cannot bound an actor you have not identified. And the E: **Escalation is the decision, not the outcome**. Most triaged cases close at E. Risk rates the case and recommends a move; the Escalation decision makes the close-or-escalate call on the evidence and records it. The thinking traps (anchoring, confirmation bias and their relatives) have their own page, <a href="/docs/cognitive-traps/">Cognitive traps in triage</a>.

<a id="case-a"></a>

### One alert, seven questions

Here is Case A, the finance-team phishing intrusion that threads through the worked examples, one line per letter:

- **A.** At 09:11 UTC on Tuesday 3 March 2026, a <DefineTerm term="SIEM (concept)" display="SIEM" /> rule fires on dlin's finance laptop: a command pasted into the Run dialog from a fake verification page started a hidden, encoded PowerShell download cradle that is beaconing to a domain registered four days earlier. By 09:24 it is validated: a true positive, malicious, at High confidence, and not contained by any control.
- **S.** dlin, her laptop and her federated cloud identity are primary. Three other finance users received the same message and stay secondary until their opens are checked.
- **S.** dlin can read a cloud finance platform that holds vendor-contact records of people in the EU. Regulated data is in reach, so the boundary takes in the platform's audit logs, and compliance and the data protection officer are engaged.
- **U.** The stage read dlin's browser cookies, an outside address replayed her session against the platform at 10:41, and at 10:52 a second finance user opened the same lure; the chain ran on that laptop without raising an alert.
- **R.** Impact High, likelihood High, confidence High: P1, with a recommendation to escalate now.
- **E.** Escalate to IR at P1. Criterion 1 (confirmed malicious activity not fully contained by a control) was met at 09:24, so the <a href="/docs/escalation/criteria/">escalation tripwire</a> called for an early Escalation decision then. The record shows the decision at 14:00; the worked examples show what the delay cost.
- **D.** The record is finalized and linked to IR's report, including the detection gap: the rule's network leg read only the web proxy, so the off-network laptop never alerted.

<Callout variant="warn" title="Triage is not incident response">
Triage produces the close-or-escalate **decision**; IR runs the **response** once a case is escalated. They are different jobs even when one person does both: on a small team, the escalation decision is the moment you change hats, and it still deserves a written handoff to your future self. **<a href="/docs/escalation/triage-vs-ir/">Triage vs. IR</a>** draws the full line.
</Callout>

## Who this is for, and what you should be able to do afterward

</div>

- **Audience:** security operations center (SOC) analysts from new hire through senior, the leads who train and review them, and detection engineers who want to know what happens downstream of their rules.
- **Prerequisites:** working familiarity with alerts, log sources, and an <DefineTerm term="Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR)" display="EDR" /> or SIEM. The glossaries backfill the rest, and every term ASSURED coins is defined in the <a href="/docs/glossaries/assured/">ASSURED terms glossary</a>.
- **Afterward you can:** validate the signal, identify every entity, draw a defensible boundary, build a confidence-labeled evidence chain, rate impact and likelihood with a stated confidence, make and record the close-or-escalate decision, and leave a record a reviewer can reconstruct without you. You can also run the <a href="/docs/fast-path/">depth ladder</a>, including a five-minute Level 0 close you can defend.
- **Reading time:** about 9 hours straight through, or 10 to 14 hours for a careful first read at study pace with the quizzes and worked examples. Each chapter takes one to two hours at that pace; <a href="/docs/start/#learning-triage">Start here</a> lists the reading time for each chapter.
- **Where to go by role:** analysts start with the chapters, in order. Trainers start with <a href="/docs/teaching/">Teaching ASSURED</a>, SOC leads with <a href="/docs/adoption/">Adopting ASSURED in your SOC</a>. Everyone can practice with the <a href="/docs/exercises/">hands-on exercises</a>.

**The loop is intentional.** Risk findings often send the analyst back to Uncover for more evidence, and Uncover sometimes reveals subjects the alert never mentioned. A new entity re-enters Subject briefly (run the four dimensions on it), and Scope then redraws the boundary.

<div class="intro-section-head">
  <span class="section-overline">Goals of the methodology</span>

## What ASSURED is designed to deliver

</div>

Three aims, none of them measured yet; <a href="/docs/development/">Development and evidence</a> says what has and has not been established.

- **Strategic automation:** enrichment, correlation and assembling the scoring inputs can be handed to tooling, while the phase boundaries keep human judgment where it is needed. Accountability is never delegated. Execution may be, at Level 0 parity, under the conditions on the <a href="/docs/ai-triage/">AI-assisted triage page</a>.
- **Process consistency:** two analysts working the same alert produce outputs a reviewer can compare line by line, because the inputs and deliverables are named.
- **Scales with the team:** nothing in the phase structure assumes a team size or a tool stack. A one-person SOC runs all seven phases itself; a multi-shift team passes the Documentation record between shifts. <a href="/docs/operating-variants/">Operating variants</a> covers solo SOCs, shift handovers and provider-run queues.

How long each phase takes, by experience level, and why most alerts never need the full arc, is on <a href="/docs/fast-path/#time-per-phase-by-experience-level">the fast path</a>.

<div class="intro-section-head">
  <span class="section-overline">Where this fits</span>

## Why ASSURED, and why now

</div>

<DefineTerm term="Incident Response" display="Incident-response" /> frameworks treat triage as one step inside a larger lifecycle. NIST SP 800-61r3 (April 2025) states it as outcomes and leaves the how-to to each organization; SANS PICERL (Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, Lessons learned) and the newer Dynamic Approach to Incident Response (DAIR) each give it a single phase; <DefineTerm term="MITRE ATT&CK" /> catalogs adversary behavior once you know what you are looking for. The <a href="/docs/standards-mapping/">standards mapping</a> shows which CSF 2.0 outcomes and which ISO/IEC 27001 control (A.5.25) the method serves, and what record proves each. <DefineTerm term="Event Triage" display="Event triage" />, the alert-by-alert work of deciding what you are looking at, is still often learned informally on the job. ASSURED expands that one step into a per-alert workflow: scaffolding for newer analysts, a checklist for experienced ones (the printable <a href="/docs/reference/">one-page reference</a>), and a shared vocabulary for handoff and review. The <a href="/docs/positioning/">related-work map</a> sets out the neighbors and a crosswalk to the standards, and <a href="/docs/development/">Development and evidence</a> covers the framework's origin and its validation limits.

<KeyTakeaway title="Recommended path">
On a first read, take the letters in order: A, S, S, U, R, E, D. Experienced analysts can take the <a href="/docs/teaching/#routing-with-a-pre-assessment">twelve-item pre-assessment</a> first and read only the pages for the items they miss. After that, jump to whatever phase the day's case demands; every chapter and glossary stands alone as a reference.
</KeyTakeaway>
