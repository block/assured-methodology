ASSURED did not invent triage, and the field around it is full of work worth reading. This page is the map: what each neighboring framework, standard, course, and thinking tool actually covers, where it is excellent, and where ASSURED's narrower contribution fits alongside it. If a claim on this page makes you want to read the source instead, follow the link; that is the point of a related-work section.

## The claim, stated carefully

ASSURED does not claim that event triage was undocumented, unstudied, or unteachable before this project. CERT/SEI named "Triage Events" as its own incident-management process in 2004, the FIRST CSIRT Services Framework lists event qualification and incident triage as services, and Investigation Theory directly teaches vendor-agnostic investigation and alert-triage skills. Incident-response standards, books, courses, research, and thinking tools cover substantial parts of the work.

The defensible contribution is narrower and concrete: **ASSURED publishes one open, named, versioned operational workflow for the work between an alert firing and the documented close-or-escalate decision.** The <a href="/docs/intro/">introduction</a> sets out the method; its templates, exercises, version history, and citation record are public. That positive contribution can be inspected; a universal claim that no other methodology exists cannot.

## The lifecycles and standards

<div class="pos-list">
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>NIST SP 800-61r3 <span class="pos-item__kind">incident-response recommendations</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> NIST's incident-response guidance, <a href="https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/61/r3/final" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">SP 800-61r3</a> (<em>Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management: A CSF 2.0 Community Profile</em>). It was published in April 2025, replaces r2 (2012, withdrawn on 3 April 2025), and is restructured around the CSF 2.0 Functions. It is a set of recommendations, not a mandatory standard. The revision deliberately dropped r2's step-by-step guidance, stating that the details of how to perform incident response change so often and vary so much that capturing them in a single static publication is no longer feasible, and it points readers to NIST's Incident Response project page and online mappings instead.</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> that shift is a strong argument for something like ASSURED existing. r3 recommends what an IR program should achieve, not how to achieve it, and tells organizations to use the incident response life cycle framework or model that suits them best. Triage still appears inside r3's model, as outcomes: DE.AE-02 to DE.AE-08 (analyze, correlate, estimate impact and scope, integrate threat intelligence, declare incidents against defined criteria) and RS.MA-02 ("Incident reports are triaged and validated"). ASSURED works inside those Detect and Respond outcomes, not before them: it is one published way to reach them for a single alert. It ends at the incident-declaration point (DE.AE-08), where executing the incident response plan (RS.MA-01) takes over: the <a href="/docs/escalation/handoff/">handoff</a>.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>ISO/IEC 27035 <span class="pos-item__kind">international standard</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> the international standard for information security incident management. <a href="https://www.iso.org/standard/78973.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ISO/IEC 27035-1:2023</a> (<em>Principles and process</em>) sets out five phases: Plan and prepare, Detect and report, Assess and decide, Respond, and Learn lessons. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 control A.5.25, "Assessment and decision on information security events", is the related control auditors test, which is why many ISO 27001-certified and non-US SOCs work to this model.</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> Assess and decide is the event-versus-incident decision point, named as a phase of its own. The standard says what that phase must accomplish; ASSURED is one way to perform it for each alert, with the close-or-escalate decision recorded so an auditor can follow it.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>CERT/SEI and FIRST CSIRT models <span class="pos-item__kind">prior art, 2004 and 2019</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> the work that first defined triage as a process in its own right. Alberts, Dorofee, Killcrece, Ruefle, and Zajicek's <a href="https://resources.sei.cmu.edu/library/asset-view.cfm?assetid=7153" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>Defining Incident Management Processes for CSIRTs</em></a> (CERT/SEI, CMU/SEI-2004-TR-015) models five processes: Prepare/Sustain/Improve, Protect Infrastructure, Detect Events, Triage Events, and Respond, with explicit handoffs from Detect to Triage and from Triage to Respond. The <a href="https://www.first.org/standards/frameworks/csirts/csirt_services_framework_v2.1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">FIRST CSIRT Services Framework v2.1</a> lists event analysis (correlation and qualification) under Information Security Event Management, and incident triage under incident report acceptance and incident analysis.</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> these models say what triage is and where it sits in a team's work, at the level of a CSIRT's process map. ASSURED works one level down: the per-alert sequence, its required outputs, and the evidence standard for the decision. Its handoff to IR is the same Triage-to-Respond boundary the 2004 model drew.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>SANS DAIR <span class="pos-item__kind">IR model, free book, 2026</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> the Dynamic Approach to Incident Response, the model taught in SANS SEC504 since at least 2024 and published by Joshua Wright in September 2026 as a free CC BY 4.0 book, <a href="https://dynamicincidentresponse.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>Dynamic Incident Response: A Framework for Security Teams</em></a>. Its waypoints are Prepare, Detect, Verify and Triage, an iterative response loop (Scope, Contain, Eradicate, Recover), and Debrief. Verify and Triage covers confirming that an event is real before committing resources.</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> DAIR is the closest open neighbor. It names verification and triage as an explicit step inside an incident-response model. ASSURED expands that single step into seven phases, each with required outputs, plus a depth ladder and a close-or-escalate rule. The two are complementary: ASSURED can be read as one detailed way to run DAIR's Verify and Triage.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>SANS PICERL <span class="pos-item__kind">IR lifecycle</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> the classic six-step incident-handling process (Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, Lessons Learned) that most IR teams can still recite. SANS popularized it, building on earlier incident-handling work going back to Eugene Schultz in 1990; Patrick Kral's <a href="https://www.sans.org/white-papers/33901/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>Incident Handler's Handbook</em></a> (SANS Institute, 2012) is the usual written reference. SANS's flagship incident-handling course, SEC504, now teaches the iterative DAIR model (above).</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> triage is a slice of Identification, and PICERL does not decompose that slice. ASSURED's seven phases are, roughly, Identification expanded to full resolution, with an explicit escalation tripwire: the moment any pre-agreed <a href="/docs/escalation/criteria/">escalation criterion</a> is confirmed (supported at High confidence; for example, confirmed malicious activity not fully contained by a control), the case goes to IR from whatever phase it is in. See <a href="/docs/escalation/triage-vs-ir/">Triage vs. IR</a> for the boundary in detail.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>MITRE ATT&amp;CK <span class="pos-item__kind">knowledge base</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> the <a href="https://attack.mitre.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">public catalog</a> of adversary tactics and techniques observed in the wild, and the industry's shared vocabulary for describing attacker behavior.</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> a vocabulary, not a process. ATT&amp;CK tells you what the behavior you found is called, which adversary goal (tactic) it serves, and which groups and software have used it; it does not tell you how to run the investigation that finds it. Its tactics are deliberately unordered; for chaining techniques into a sequence, see the Center for Threat-Informed Defense's <a href="https://center-for-threat-informed-defense.github.io/attack-flow/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Attack Flow</a>. ASSURED uses ATT&amp;CK inside <a href="/docs/uncover/mitre/">Uncover</a> as the mapping layer for reconstructed chains.</p>
  </div>
</div>

    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> <a href="https://chrissanders.org/training/investigationtheory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Investigation Theory</a> is vendor-agnostic training centered on the mental models used by investigators: diagnostic inquiry, evidence, questions, decisions, data transformation, playbooks, bias, and reporting. Investigation Theory goes deeper into cognition and the transferable craft of investigation. ASSURED is narrower and more procedural: it defines the sequence and required artifacts for carrying one event from alert to close or incident-response handoff. ASSURED was not derived from Investigation Theory; see <a href="/docs/development/#relationship-to-investigation-theory">Development and evidence</a> for the provenance record.</p>

## Playbooks, runbooks and SOAR: where ASSURED fits

Most SOCs already run vendor or government playbooks and SOAR flows, so the first question a lead asks is whether ASSURED replaces them. It does not. Playbooks say what to do for a scenario; ASSURED says what every case must establish and record, whatever the scenario. Keep the playbooks, and use the phases to structure the reasoning and the record around each one.

<div class="pos-list">
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>Open playbook libraries <span class="pos-item__kind">government and vendor playbooks</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> free, operational runbooks such as CISA's <a href="https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/federal-government-cybersecurity-incident-and-vulnerability-response-playbooks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Federal Government Cybersecurity Incident and Vulnerability Response Playbooks</a> (2021), Microsoft's incident-response playbooks, and the AWS Security Incident Response Guide. They are the free alternatives a SOC lead will actually compare ASSURED against.</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> playbooks are scenario-specific and often tool-specific: they say what to do for phishing, or for a compromised cloud key, in a given environment. ASSURED is the method underneath them, the same seven questions for every alert.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>Automated and AI-assisted triage <span class="pos-item__kind">2026 platforms</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> SOAR enrichment, risk-based alerting that reduces volume before an alert reaches a person, vendor incident correlation, and the AI SOC agents now built into SIEM and XDR platforms, which draft or carry out triage steps on their own.</p>

Accountability is never delegated; execution may be, at Level 0 parity, under the governed machine-close conditions on the <a href="/docs/ai-triage/#governed-automation">AI-assisted triage</a> page, with QA rates, pause triggers and the kill switch on <a href="/docs/ai-governance/">Governing automated triage</a>.</p>

### Playbook, pattern entry, ASSURED phase

Three artifacts overlap on a triage desk, and each answers a different question:

| | Playbook or runbook | Pattern entry | ASSURED phase |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Answers | What do we do for this scenario, in this environment? | Is this alert the documented benign behavior, and how do we know? | What must this case establish next? |
| Covers | One scenario (phishing, a leaked cloud key) | One detection's known-benign behavior | Every case |
| Owned by | IR or SOC engineering | A named owner, with a second person's approval | The analyst working the case |
| Produces | Steps taken and actions run | A Level 0 close that cites the entry and its version | The phase deliverable in the case record |
| Changes when | The scenario or the tooling changes | A rule change, an observed abuse, or a reversal threshold | The method's version changes |

A playbook's steps land inside phases. "Check the sender's reputation" is Alert validation, "pull the message trace" is Subject and Scope evidence, and "reset the password" is a response action that follows the Escalation decision. Map an existing playbook onto the seven phases and the gaps usually show at the end: many playbooks have no recorded decision and no closure record. The <a href="/docs/fast-path/">fast path</a> describes the pattern library and the depth ladder that pattern entries feed.

### Converting a playbook step into a discriminator

A playbook step names a check; a discriminator names the result that separates the benign behavior from the threat it mimics. "Confirm the process is the approved backup agent" is a step. The discriminators behind it might be "parent process is the backup service, destinations fall within the entry's documented set, no reads of credential stores". To convert a step:

1. Name the threat the benign behavior resembles.
2. Write the observable that differs between the two, and the authoritative source that shows it.
3. State the failing result and what it triggers: one failed discriminator turns a Level 0 case into a Level 1 case.
4. Prefer observables an attacker cannot cheaply forge (exact destination, parent process, extension or plugin ID, group membership). Names, paths and ports are cheap to fake, and a signer or install path does not vouch for code that a signed host runs.

### What SOAR may automate under Level 0 parity

- **Enrichment and assembly, always:** identity lookups, asset criticality, sandbox verdicts, timeline assembly. These pre-answer Subject and Scope questions. They are input to the ladder, never a verdict.
- **Pre-authorized containment, when the protocol lists it:** reversible actions only, after the preservation step, under the <a href="/docs/escalation/triage-vs-ir/#pre-authorized">pre-authorized immediate actions</a> agreed with IR. Non-reversible actions (a credential reset, an OAuth grant revocation) need IR or owner approval unless the protocol lists them for that scenario.
- **Closes, only at <a href="/docs/ai-governance/#level-0-parity">Level 0 parity</a>:** the pattern entry carries a machine-close flag, every discriminator and the eligibility gate are evaluated by code, humans sample the closes, reversal and miss rates are tracked, and a kill switch and a named owner exist.
- **Never:** a verdict above Level 0 parity, or a Level 0 close of a grouped case in which any member fails eligibility. The record names the automation and the pattern version either way.

## The thinking tools

<div class="pos-list">
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>OODA <span class="pos-item__kind">decision cycle</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> John Boyd's Observe, Orient, Decide, Act loop, developed in his briefings (summarized in <a href="https://www.danford.net/boyd/essence.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">"The Essence of Winning and Losing"</a>, 1995), and the general-purpose model for decision-making under adversarial pressure.</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> ASSURED is a domain-specific instantiation. Alert through Uncover observe and orient. Risk completes the orientation: it rates impact and likelihood, states its confidence, sets the priority, and recommends a move. The Escalation decision is the decide step, where the close or the escalation is made and recorded. The close, the handoff, and any pre-authorized containment are the act, and the Scope → Uncover → Risk loop is the re-orientation cycle. OODA explains why the loop exists; ASSURED specifies what each turn of it produces.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>ACH <span class="pos-item__kind">analytic technique</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> Richards J. Heuer Jr.'s Analysis of Competing Hypotheses, from Chapter 8 of <a href="https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/books-monographs/psychology-of-intelligence-analysis-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>Psychology of Intelligence Analysis</em></a> (CIA Center for the Study of Intelligence, 1999; free): enumerate the explanations, test evidence against all of them, and privilege disconfirmation over confirmation.</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> Uncover's <a href="/docs/uncover/correlation/">hypothesis ledger</a> is ACH scaled to triage speed: competing explanations held open, negatives recorded, refuted stays refuted. If the ledger feels useful, ACH is the deeper well it draws from.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>The Diamond Model <span class="pos-item__kind">intrusion-analysis model</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> the 2013 <a href="https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/citations/ADA586960" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Caltagirone, Pendergast, and Betz paper</a> modeling every intrusion event as adversary, capability, infrastructure, and victim, with pivots along the edges.</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> a lens for the analysis inside Uncover, especially attribution and infrastructure pivoting. ASSURED covers the workflow around the lens: when to pick it up, what the output feeds, and when to stop pivoting.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pos-item">
    <h3>OSCAR <span class="pos-item__kind">forensics methodology</span></h3>
    <p><strong>What it is:</strong> OSCAR (Obtain information, Strategize, Collect evidence, Analyze, Report), from Sherri Davidoff and Jonathan Ham's <a href="https://www.informit.com/store/network-forensics-tracking-hackers-through-cyberspace-9780132565097" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><em>Network Forensics: Tracking Hackers through Cyberspace</em></a> (Prentice Hall, 2012), which some SOCs apply to alert investigations.</p>
    <p><strong>How it relates:</strong> the nearest neighbor by shape. Obtain information and Strategize overlap Alert, Subject, and Scope, and Collect evidence, Analyze, and Report overlap Uncover and Documentation. What OSCAR does not carry are the triage-specific decisions: the validation verdict, Risk's ratings and priority, escalation criteria, and the depth ladder. The two are compatible: an OSCAR-trained analyst will recognize Uncover immediately.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout variant="warn" title="A name adjacency worth knowing">
The UK's National Cyber Security Centre operates <a href="https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/section/products-services/assured-services" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">"Assured" schemes</a>: NCSC-assured services, including the assured Cyber Incident Response and Cyber Incident Exercising schemes, consultancies, and NCSC Assured Training. A search for "assured incident response" therefore finds NCSC-assured IR providers first. The US Defense Information Systems Agency's ACAS (Assured Compliance Assessment Solution) is a vulnerability-scanning program, also unrelated. This ASSURED methodology has no affiliation with the NCSC, DISA, or other "Assured" products and schemes; the shared word is coincidence. When searching, "ASSURED triage" or "ASSURED methodology" finds this project.
</Callout>

<KeyTakeaway>The field already contains strong investigation training, research, IR lifecycles and standards, CSIRT process models, playbooks, books, and thinking tools. ASSURED does not claim to originate investigative reasoning or triage. It contributes one open per-alert workflow that a team can teach, adopt, review, and revise.</KeyTakeaway>
