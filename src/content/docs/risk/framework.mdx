# The four inputs

<div class="risk-inputs">
  <div class="ri"><h4>💥 Potential impact</h4><p>If the finding is real, what is the worst plausible outcome? Operational, financial, regulatory, reputational. The impact dimension is independent of how confident the analyst is.</p></div>
  <div class="ri"><h4>🎭 Actor sophistication</h4><p>What level of capability does the evidence suggest? Commodity malware is one tier; targeted attacks with custom tooling are another. Sophistication informs urgency and the response posture.</p></div>
  <div class="ri"><h4>🧭 Context</h4><p>Who is affected, what data is at stake, what regulations apply, and what the broader environment looks like. The same evidence can have different weight depending on context.</p></div>
  <div class="ri"><h4>🚪 Escalation criteria</h4><p>Pre-defined thresholds that, if met, trigger escalation regardless of analyst judgment. Examples: lateral movement confirmed, regulated data accessed, critical asset compromised.</p></div>
</div>

# CVSS as one input

<DefineTerm term="CVSS">Common Vulnerability Scoring System. A numerical score (0 to 10) that describes the severity of a vulnerability based on exploitability, impact, and contextual factors. CVSS is useful for vulnerability prioritization but should not be the sole driver of triage decisions because it does not capture environment-specific context.</DefineTerm> is a useful input to Risk decisions, especially when a known vulnerability is part of the picture. The score answers a specific question (how severe is this vulnerability in the abstract) but does not answer the Risk question (how much does this matter in our environment for this case).

<Callout variant="info" title="When CVSS misleads">
A CVSS 9.8 on an asset that is not exposed, not in use, or already mitigated by compensating controls deserves a different response than the same score on a production system. Use CVSS as input, then weight it by the context Subject and Scope provided.
</Callout>
