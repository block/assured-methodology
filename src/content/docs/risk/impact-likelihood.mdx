## Evaluating impact

Impact asks: if this is real, how bad is it? Four factors cover it, each assessed on its own, then combined.

<div class="impact-grid">
  <div class="impact-card">
    <span class="impact-card__num">01</span>
    <h3>Data sensitivity</h3>
    <p>Value and sensitivity of data involved (personal, financial, health). Exposure can lead to identity theft, fraud, legal consequences. Different categories (public → restricted) carry different risk weights when compromised. PII, PHI, payment card data, intellectual property, and authentication credentials sit at the top tier.</p>
    <p class="impact-card__note">Volume also matters. A breach of thousands of customer records is a different scale from a single record, even at the same classification.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="impact-card">
    <span class="impact-card__num">02</span>
    <h3>Operational disruption</h3>
    <p>How the event affects the organization's ability to function. Direct availability issues, productivity losses, customer-facing service disruption. The criticality of affected systems to core business functions drives this.</p>
    <p class="impact-card__note">Recovery time objectives (RTO) and recovery point objectives (RPO) anchor the operational evaluation. Threatening to exceed these thresholds raises priority materially.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="impact-card">
    <span class="impact-card__num">03</span>
    <h3>Regulatory &amp; compliance</h3>
    <p>Industry and geography-specific legal obligations. GDPR and HIPAA set statutory notification timelines and define what counts as a reportable breach. PCI DSS requires an incident response plan that includes notifying the payment brands and acquirers (Req. 12.10.1); the deadlines themselves come from each card brand's rules and the acquirer contract, and they run from suspicion, not confirmation. US state breach laws add their own clocks (California's SB 446: 30 calendar days from discovery), and so do sector and EU regimes such as NIS2, DORA, and NYDFS Part 500, some of them measured in hours. SOX adds financial-reporting control obligations, and for US public companies the SEC rule adds a Form 8-K disclosure due four business days after the company determines an incident is material. See <a href="/docs/scope/regulatory/">Scope's regulatory page</a> for the full map.</p>
    <p class="impact-card__note">Different regulatory regimes have different thresholds for materiality and different notification clocks. Scope identified which regimes may apply; Risk records whether the technical evidence plausibly meets a threshold and hands that evidence to compliance and counsel, who make the determination.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="impact-card">
    <span class="impact-card__num">04</span>
    <h3>Reputational</h3>
    <p>How the event affects stakeholder perceptions if it became public. Customer trust, partner relationships, investor confidence, market positioning. Reputational damage often outlasts direct operational impact and is harder to quantify and remediate.</p>
    <p class="impact-card__note">Factors include visibility of affected systems, data sensitivity, the organization's existing public profile, and whether the incident suggests negligence vs. sophisticated targeting.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout variant="info" title="How these feed the RATM">
These four factors are the evidence for the two impact dimensions of the Risk-Based Alert Triage Matrix (RATM) on the <a href="/docs/risk/framework/#ratm">priority matrix page</a>. Data sensitivity and the asset's operational role feed **asset criticality**; operational disruption, regulatory exposure, and reputational harm feed **business impact**. Impact on the matrix is the higher of the two, so a triggered notification clock alone can make impact High. Confirmed spread raises impact only through asset criticality: when the footprint reaches a more valuable asset, score that asset.
</Callout>

## Evaluating likelihood

Likelihood is one question: **how probable is it that this activity is malicious or unauthorized?** It is an estimate, made from the evidence:

- **The evidence sets the ceiling.** No factor raises likelihood above what the evidence supports.
- **The four factors below calibrate within it:** by at most one step, and only with the reason written in the case.
- **Whether an attack will continue or spread is scored elsewhere.** Footprint feeds impact (through asset criticality) and the lateral-movement escalation criterion, not this axis.

Beside the estimate, record **confidence**: how well the evidence and reasoning support it, scored on the <a href="/docs/documentation/standards/#confidence">shared confidence rubric</a>. The two lines answer different questions, and the record keeps both. The full reduction from the four RATM dimensions to the two matrix axes is on the <a href="/docs/risk/framework/">priority matrix page</a>.

One scoring rule prevents the quietest failure: **unknown is not Low.** Low means the evidence was examined and leans benign. When the evidence cannot be examined (telemetry is missing, a source is unqueried, or a join failed), likelihood cannot read Low: it reads Medium at minimum, confidence reads Low, and the gap goes in the open questions. No factor substitutes for evidence either: an <DefineTerm term="Advanced Persistent Threat (APT)" display="APT" />-plausible story never rises above what the evidence supports. The framework page's reduction and override rules depend on this distinction: "checked and clean" and "could not check" must never land in the same matrix cell.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why we call it likelihood, and record confidence separately">
Intelligence analysis keeps two judgments apart: how likely something is, and how much confidence the analyst has in that judgment (US Intelligence Community Directive 203, Analytic Standards). ASSURED follows the same split. Likelihood is the estimate that this activity is malicious or unauthorized; confidence is the quality of the evidence and reasoning behind the estimate. Strong evidence that activity is benign reads Low likelihood at High confidence. A plausible but unexamined story reads Medium likelihood at Low confidence. Keeping the lines apart is what lets a reviewer tell weak evidence from strong evidence of benign activity, and it matches the estimative language (likely, very likely; low, moderate, high confidence) analysts already meet in threat-intelligence reporting.
</Callout>

<div class="likelihood-grid">
  <div class="likelihood-card">
    <span class="likelihood-card__num">01</span>
    <h3>Threat actor sophistication</h3>
    <p>Skill level and resources behind the activity. Custom tooling, multi-stage operations, and infrastructure investment are evidence of intent and raise the threat actor intent dimension; they do not by themselves raise confidence that this finding is real. Commodity is not harmless: an opportunistic infostealer still takes working credentials.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="likelihood-card">
    <span class="likelihood-card__num">02</span>
    <h3>Exploitability</h3>
    <p>Whether the attack targets known, easily exploitable vulnerabilities or requires advanced techniques. Public exploits, low complexity, and broad applicability make success more plausible. Patch status and compensating controls temper it: an exploit for a vulnerability this host does not have cannot have succeeded here.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="likelihood-card">
    <span class="likelihood-card__num">03</span>
    <h3>Base rates and history</h3>
    <p>How often has this detection, on this asset class, produced confirmed true positives, and how often has this organization confirmed this kind of activity before? A rule that is right 2% of the time sets a low prior; a recurring, confirmed campaign sets a higher one. Cite counts from case records, not memories (see <a href="/docs/cognitive-traps/">Thinking traps</a>: availability bias). History calibrates; it never substitutes for this case's evidence.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="likelihood-card">
    <span class="likelihood-card__num">04</span>
    <h3>Security control coverage</h3>
    <p>Effectiveness of firewalls, intrusion detection, endpoint protection, identity controls. Gaps increase the chance of successful compromise; layered, well-maintained controls reduce it. Compensating controls temper exploitability: did a control block, quarantine, or log the attempt?</p>
  </div>
</div>

### Likelihood indicators at a glance

Evidence quality is the first row because it is the ceiling; the four calibration rows move the score within it, by at most one step, never above it. That is the <a href="/docs/risk/framework/">priority matrix page's</a> reduction rule, read as a table.

<div class="likelihood-table">
  <div class="lt-row lt-row--header"><span>Factor</span><span>Low likelihood</span><span>Medium likelihood</span><span>High likelihood</span></div>
  <div class="lt-row"><span class="lt-row__label">Evidence quality (ceiling)</span><span>Examined and affirmatively leans benign</span><span>Single-source or partially examined; or the evidence could not be examined (gap recorded, confidence Low)</span><span>Multiple independent sources corroborate malicious behavior</span></div>
  <div class="lt-row"><span class="lt-row__label">Threat actor</span><span>Opportunistic, untargeted, limited resources</span><span>Commodity tooling with some targeting</span><span>Targeted, persistent, well-resourced, attributed with corroboration</span></div>
  <div class="lt-row"><span class="lt-row__label">Exploitability</span><span>Target not vulnerable, or exploitation needs access the actor does not hold</span><span>Exploitable with effort; preconditions partly met</span><span>Simple exploitation, public exploits available, target confirmed vulnerable</span></div>
  <div class="lt-row"><span class="lt-row__label">Security controls</span><span>Multiple overlapping controls, well-maintained, and they logged or blocked the attempt</span><span>Controls present, with known gaps on this path</span><span>Limited or outdated controls, known gaps</span></div>
  <div class="lt-row"><span class="lt-row__label">Base rates and history</span><span>Detection rarely confirms; no confirmed history of this activity here</span><span>Detection sometimes confirms; similar activity confirmed here before</span><span>Detection usually confirms; recurring confirmed incidents or an active campaign</span></div>
</div>

<Callout variant="warn" title="Common likelihood mistake">
Letting impact drive likelihood. "This would be really bad, therefore it is probably real." That is backwards. Evaluate likelihood from the evidence, impact from the potential consequences, and then combine them deliberately. Each dimension answers a different question. Two more traps bite at this phase: anchoring on a severity score that arrived with the alert, from a vendor console or an AI assistant, and availability bias from the last memorable incident. See <a href="/docs/cognitive-traps/">Thinking traps</a> and <a href="/docs/ai-triage/">AI-assisted triage</a>.
</Callout>

## Producing the assessment

The Risk output combines the two axes into a defensible priority and a recommended move. The assessment should include:

<div class="verdict-list">
  <div class="verdict-item">
    <h3>Impact score</h3>
    <p>High / medium / low for asset criticality and for business impact, each with a one-sentence rationale drawn from the four impact factors. Impact is the higher of the two. The combined impact selects the row of the triage matrix the case reads from; at High, it also puts the case on the high-impact clock.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="verdict-item">
    <h3>Likelihood score</h3>
    <p>High / medium / low: the estimate that the activity is malicious or unauthorized, anchored on the evidence and calibrated by sophistication, exploitability, control coverage, and base rates and history.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="verdict-item">
    <h3>Confidence</h3>
    <p>High / medium / low on the shared confidence rubric, as its own named line, so a reviewer can tell "weak evidence" apart from "strong evidence of benign activity." Say whether any dimension is Unknown.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="verdict-item">
    <h3>Combined priority</h3>
    <p>The matrix cell and its band, P1 to P4. Drives escalation timing and resource allocation. Not a number; a defended judgment with named inputs.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="verdict-item">
    <h3>Override applied</h3>
    <p>None, escalation tripwire, high-impact clock, or documented downgrade, with the reason.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="verdict-item">
    <h3>Recommended move</h3>
    <p>The action the band calls for, in one line: escalate now, escalate within 4 hours, keep working to a named re-score trigger, or documented close. Risk recommends; the Escalation decision decides.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="verdict-item">
    <h3>Open questions</h3>
    <p>What the analyst could not resolve, and what new evidence would change the assessment. This is what makes it defensible later.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Open questions are not weakness. Recording them is the difference between "the analyst was confident" and "the analyst knew what they did not know." The second is what survives audit, <DefineTerm term="Post-Mortem" display="post-mortem" />, and review; the first is what creates incident-response gaps months later, when the open question turns out to have mattered.

<KeyTakeaway>
Impact and likelihood are independent. Combine them deliberately, record confidence beside them, and record the rationale for each. The assessment is not a number; it is a defended judgment with named inputs the next analyst, the audit team, and the post-mortem can verify.
</KeyTakeaway>
