## The analyst's regulatory job: recognize, timestamp, preserve, hand off

*Law as of October 2026. Regulatory text changes faster than technique; check dates against your compliance team's current list.*

A triage analyst is not a lawyer and does not need to be one. The regulatory work in Scope is four moves:

1. **Recognize the data class.** Personal data, cardholder data, health information, financial-reporting systems. The moment a regulated class is plausibly in reach of the compromise, its framework becomes a candidate: tag the case and route the applicability call to the owner. Reach is a Scope flag; regulated data plausibly accessed by the activity under review is what moves a case to Level 2 on the [depth ladder](/docs/fast-path/).
2. **Commit the timestamps.** Most clocks run from *awareness*, *discovery* or *suspicion*, not from confirmation. Record in UTC when the SOC first had reasonable cause to believe regulated data was involved, including the time an external notice reached the organization (see [Other intake](/docs/alert/intake/#external-notifications)), and when each owner was engaged.
3. **Preserve.** Delete nothing, and find every rotation, teardown and cleanup job that will destroy relevant data before the case can use it. Request a retention extension or hold through the authorized path (the owning team under your documented preservation procedure, or counsel's hold once litigation or a regulatory response is plausible), and record the request and its time; when containment is coming, see [Preserve before you contain](/docs/escalation/preserve/).
4. **Get the owner into the case.** The privacy officer or <DefineTerm term="Data Protection Officer (DPO)" />, counsel, compliance, or the owner of the acquirer relationship (the bank that processes your card payments). They interpret the obligation; you give them the facts and the timestamps. None of this waits for a final verdict, and if the activity is confirmed and regulated data is in its path, the high-impact [escalation criterion](/docs/escalation/criteria/) may already be met.

<Callout variant="warn" title="This page is triage guidance, not legal advice">
Which obligations apply depends on jurisdiction, contracts, and your organization's role (controller or processor, covered entity or business associate, merchant or service provider). This page is about recognition: knowing when to raise your hand, and how fast. Your privacy and legal teams own the rest.
</Callout>

<Reveal summary="The four moves, run on Case A" variant="example">

The regulatory pass on [Case A, the finance-team intrusion](/docs/scope/example/#case-a), takes minutes:

1. **Recognize.** The compromised user's federated identity can reach a cloud finance reporting platform that feeds vendor payments and stores vendor-contact records. Two regulated classes are plausibly in reach: cardholder data (PCI DSS, *if* the platform touches card data at all; compliance owns that question) and personal data of vendor contacts in the EU (GDPR, *if* an Article 3 nexus attaches: the DPO's call). "Can reach" is enough to tag the case; no platform activity has been seen yet. If the company is publicly listed, the platform is also a likely SOX-relevant system, so finance compliance is flagged too.
2. **Commit the timestamps.** The alert (09:11 UTC) and the moment the analyst recognized regulated data was plausibly in reach (09:42). If GDPR applies, later moments (such as finding an attacker session on the platform) join these as awareness candidates; the DPO, not the analyst, decides which one counts.
3. **Preserve.** The platform's audit logs are requested and held past their rotation schedule, the stage's on-disk copy `%TEMP%\upd.ps1` and its hash stay intact, and automated cleanup on the proxy logs is paused for the case window.
4. **Get the owners in.** Compliance (who own the PCI question and the acquirer relationship, if there is one) and the DPO join the case during Scope at 09:45, hours before Uncover confirms anything on the platform.

The analyst decides neither whether the cardholder data environment (CDE) was crossed nor whether notification is required. The same pass on [Case B](/docs/scope/example/#case-b), the Cursor benign true positive, concludes "no regulated data in reach; source code is sensitive but not regulated," and records it. A documented "none" is a regulatory boundary too.

</Reveal>

## Regimes an analyst should recognize

Every regime asks three things of triage: what you may touch, what records you must leave, and which clock, if any, starts. This page teaches six. The [regulatory clock card](/docs/scope/clocks/) carries the full recognition table, including NIS2, DORA and the US sector rules, with recipients and primary sources.

| Regime | Recognize it when | Clock starts at | First deadline | Owner to engage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [GDPR](#gdpr) | Personal data, and an Article 3 nexus to the EU | Awareness | 72 hours to the supervisory authority, where feasible | DPO |
| [California](#ccpa) | California residents' personal information | Discovery | 30 calendar days to residents | Privacy and legal |
| [PCI DSS](#pci) | Account data, or a system in or connected to the CDE | Suspicion | The acquirer immediately; brands within days (Visa: three) | Acquirer-relationship owner |
| [HIPAA](#hipaa) | Protected Health Information (PHI) at a covered entity or business associate | Discovery | Without unreasonable delay, 60 days at most | Privacy officer |
| [SOX](#sox) | Financial-reporting systems at a public company | No clock of its own | Preservation and control-deficiency duties | Finance compliance |
| [SEC 8-K](#sec) | A possibly material incident at a US public company | Materiality determination | Four business days | Disclosure committee |

## GDPR

GDPR applies where one of Article 3's nexus tests is met: processing in the context of an EU/EEA establishment (Art. 3(1)), offering goods or services to people in the EU (Art. 3(2)(a)), or monitoring their behavior (Art. 3(2)(b)). A non-EU company that merely holds an EU visitor's data is not automatically in scope. That call is the DPO's; the analyst records the candidate facts (whose data, where the subjects are). Personal data is broad (IP addresses and device identifiers usually qualify), and a breach includes loss or alteration, not only disclosure (Art. 4(12)).

- **Investigate, minimally.** Strictly necessary security monitoring is a recognized legitimate interest (Recital 49; Art. 6(1)(f)), subject to the balancing test your privacy team documents. Public authorities cannot rely on legitimate interests for their own tasks; their basis is usually Art. 6(1)(c) or (e), so check with the DPO. Special-category data (health, biometrics) needs its own Art. 9(2) condition: flag it to the DPO. Pull only the data and window the case needs, and record why (Art. 5(1)(c)).
- **The clock.** The supervisory authority hears without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of awareness, unless the breach is unlikely to result in risk (Art. 33(1)). A late notification must give the reasons for the delay, and details may follow in phases (Art. 33(4)). Every breach, notified or not, goes in the controller's internal breach record (Art. 33(5)), which is where a documented "none" lives. A processor notifies its controller without undue delay (Art. 33(2)), and that notice generally starts the controller's 72 hours. UK GDPR works the same way, through the ICO (see the [clock card](/docs/scope/clocks/#eu-and-uk)). (The proposed EU Digital Omnibus would raise the threshold and lengthen the deadline; as of October 2026 it is not adopted, and 72 hours is the law.)
- **Cross-border handling.** An analyst outside the EU/EEA pulling or even remotely viewing raw logs is a Chapter V transfer: flag it so privacy can confirm the mechanism (an adequacy decision, <DefineTerm term="Standard Contractual Clauses (SCC)" display="Standard Contractual Clauses" /> or Binding Corporate Rules). Employee-monitoring rules can also limit who may examine an employee's data (see [insider analysis](/docs/subject/insider-analysis/)).
- **Scope records.** The awareness candidates, the categories of personal data involved, and a rough count of data subjects.

<Callout variant="warn" title="The 72-hour clock starts at awareness, not at confirmation">
<DefineTerm term="Awareness (GDPR)" display="Awareness" /> is a reasonable degree of certainty that a security incident has compromised personal data ([EDPB Guidelines 9/2022](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-92022-personal-data-breach-notification-under_en), paras. 31 and 34). A short, prompt check on whether a breach occurred does not start the clock; an open-ended one gets no such benefit. Record when the check started and when it reached reasonable certainty.
</Callout>

<h2 id="ccpa">California (CCPA/CPRA and the breach statute)</h2>

The breach duty comes from <a href="https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.82" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Cal. Civ. Code §1798.82</a>, which binds anyone doing business in California who owns or licenses computerized personal information, whatever the CCPA thresholds. The CCPA (as amended by the CPRA) adds consumer-rights clocks and a private right of action for certain breaches (§1798.150), which is why counsel wants facts as they develop.

- **The clock.** Since January 1, 2026 (SB 446), residents get notice within 30 calendar days of discovery or notification of the breach. Delay is allowed only for law enforcement's needs, or to determine the breach's scope and restore the system's integrity. When more than 500 residents are notified, a sample notice goes to the Attorney General within 15 days of notifying them. If you hold the data for someone else, notify the owner immediately after discovery (§1798.82(b)).
- **Rights clocks keep running.** Access, correction and deletion requests run on a 45-day clock (extendable once) that does not pause for your investigation; invoking the security exception (§1798.105(d)) is the privacy team's decision.
- **Scope records.** The discovery timestamp, the categories of personal information in reach, an estimate of affected residents, and a plain-language reason for each investigative access.

<h2 id="pci">PCI DSS</h2>

PCI DSS is mostly a contractual standard flowing from the card brands through acquiring banks to merchants (a few states, such as Nevada, write it into statute).

- **The boundary is the CDE.** Systems that store, process, or transmit account data, plus systems with unrestricted connectivity to them, form the <DefineTerm term="Cardholder Data Environment (CDE)" display="cardholder data environment (CDE)" definition="The system components, people, and processes that store, process, or transmit cardholder data or sensitive authentication data, plus system components with unrestricted connectivity to them. Connected-to and security-impacting systems are in PCI scope but are not part of the CDE." />. Scope's first PCI question is whether anything the compromised identity or host can reach sits inside it.
- **No investigator lane.** Access into the CDE during an investigation follows the same least-privilege, unique-ID and logging requirements (Req. 7, 8 and 10) as any other: use the sanctioned path (jump hosts, <DefineTerm term="Privileged Access Management (PAM)" display="PAM" />, logged sessions).
- **The evidence window you should expect.** <a href="https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/document_library/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Requirement 10.5.1</a> keeps audit logs for at least 12 months, the most recent 3 immediately available. That is the minimum look-back to expect for CDE systems; verify actual retention, and tampering, before relying on it.
- **The clock runs from suspicion.** Card-brand rules apply to suspected compromise. Visa requires immediate notice to the acquirer and a report within three calendar days; American Express, notice within 72 hours. Confirm Mastercard's rules and your acquirer agreement for the figure that binds you (see the [card-brand clocks](/docs/scope/clocks/#contractual-card-brand-and-insurance-clocks)). A confirmed compromise typically brings in a <DefineTerm term="PFI" display="PCI Forensic Investigator" definition="A PCI SSC-qualified forensic company engaged after a confirmed or suspected account data compromise to determine what happened and what data was exposed. Card brands can require a PFI engagement as a condition of continued processing." />, who can use only what you preserved.
- **Scope's PCI checklist.** Tag the case the moment account data is plausibly in reach, request that CDE logs be held the same day, and tell the acquirer-relationship owner the same day: they own the brand-notification decision.

## HIPAA

HIPAA governs PHI held by covered entities and their business associates.

- **You may investigate.** Internal security review is part of the covered entity's own health care operations (45 CFR §164.506), and the Security Rule requires incident procedures (§164.308(a)(6)). §164.512 governs disclosures to outside parties, not looking at your own logs.
- **Minimum necessary applies to you.** Query the PHI the case needs (§164.502(b)), prefer metadata and masked views, and document why fuller access was needed when it was.
- **Discovery starts the 60-day clock.** <DefineTerm term="Discovery (HIPAA)" display="Discovery" /> is the first day anyone in the workforce other than the wrongdoer knew, or with reasonable diligence would have known; the alert that starts triage can be it, so engage the privacy officer as soon as PHI is plausibly in reach. Impermissible access to unsecured PHI is presumed a breach unless the privacy officer's four-factor assessment (§164.402) shows a low probability of compromise, and that assessment does not pause the clock. HHS applies the same presumption to ransomware: encryption of ePHI by ransomware is presumed a breach unless that assessment shows a low probability of compromise, so "no exfiltration" does not clear it. Individuals hear within 60 days at the latest (§164.404). A business associate's clock runs to the covered entity (§164.410), and the agreement probably says sooner.
- **Scope records.** The discovery timestamp, the PHI types in reach and the access justifications, which join breach documentation kept for six years (§164.530(j)).

## SOX

Sarbanes-Oxley has no breach-notification clock. Its bite during an investigation is records, controls, and where findings flow.

- **An incident can be a control deficiency.** A compromise of in-scope financial systems may show that an internal control failed (Section 404), which the SOX program must evaluate.
- **Destruction is criminal; retention is scoped.** 18 U.S.C. §1519 (added by Section 802) makes it a crime for anyone to destroy or alter records to obstruct a federal matter, even one only contemplated. Rule 2-06 of Regulation S-X binds *accountants'* audit workpapers; it is not a universal seven-year rule for incident logs. Alter nothing, and let counsel scope any hold.
- **Segregation of duties survives the incident.** Use the documented access and change process for SOX-scoped systems, even when it is slower.
- **Findings flow upward.** Disclosure controls (Section 302) route incidents that could affect financial reporting to the disclosure committee, so Scope marks the case SOX-relevant early.

<h2 id="sec">SEC cyber disclosure</h2>

US public companies disclose material cybersecurity incidents on Form 8-K Item 1.05, within four business days of determining that the incident is material. The determination itself must come without unreasonable delay after discovery, and counsel makes it from your case record: scope of access, data classes, business functions affected, and when each fact was established. Write down any link to related incidents, which can be material together. The Attorney General can delay a filing on national-security or public-safety grounds (Item 1.05(c)), which only works if law enforcement was engaged early. Item 1.05 also covers incidents on third-party systems the company uses, so a vendor's breach can raise the materiality question. An incident not yet determined to be material, or one the company chooses to disclose voluntarily, goes under Item 8.01, not 1.05 (SEC staff guidance, May 2024). Separately, Regulation S-K Item 106 requires annual disclosure of cybersecurity risk management and governance in Form 10-K Item 1C, which draws on the same records of how cases like this are handled. As of October 2026, Item 1.05 is in force.

## Other clocks you may meet

Several other regimes can start a clock from the same alert, and some run in hours. In financial services or critical infrastructure, the shortest clock in the case is probably one of these.

<h3 id="nis2">NIS2</h3>

Recognize the <DefineTerm term="Network and Information Security Directive (NIS2)" display="NIS2 Directive" /> when the organization is an essential or important entity in a sector such as energy, transport, banking, health, digital infrastructure or managed ICT services, and the incident could be significant. The first deadline is an early warning to the national <DefineTerm term="Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT)" display="CSIRT" /> or competent authority within 24 hours of awareness, which leaves no room for a next-morning handoff: record when the SOC became aware and engage the owner at once. The later stages are on the [clock card](/docs/scope/clocks/#eu-and-uk).

<h3 id="dora">DORA</h3>

Recognize the <DefineTerm term="Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA)" display="Digital Operational Resilience Act" /> when the organization is an EU financial entity and an ICT incident touches clients, critical services or data. The entity classifies the incident against the regulation's criteria using your facts (clients affected, duration, data losses, critical services touched), and a major incident starts a clock measured in hours, so commit those facts with timestamps. The deadlines are on the [clock card](/docs/scope/clocks/#eu-and-uk).

**Also on the [clock card](/docs/scope/clocks/):** UK GDPR, the EU Cyber Resilience Act (manufacturers, since September 11, 2026), NYDFS 500.17 (including 24-hour notice of an extortion payment), the FTC Safeguards Rule, SEC Regulation S-P, the US banking 36-hour rule, DFARS 252.204-7012, other US state laws, and CIRCIA (not yet in force). Compliance keeps the authoritative list; your job is to know the clock exists and commit the timestamp it runs from.

## Legal holds and contractual clocks

- **<DefineTerm term="Legal Hold" display="Legal hold" />.** Once litigation or a regulatory proceeding is reasonably anticipated, a duty to preserve attaches, and spoliation (FRCP 37(e) in US federal court) can cost more than the incident. Counsel issues the hold and sets its scope; the analyst flags when a case is heading that way and lists what destroys relevant data by default (log rotation, ephemeral teardown, cleanup jobs) so it can be suspended for the identified systems.
- **Contractual clocks.** Data-processing agreements often set customer notice at 24 to 72 hours, and insurers make prompt notice a condition of coverage. Ask: whose data is this, and what did we promise them?
- **Write facts, not legal conclusions.** Notes may be read by regulators and opposing counsel. Record what you observed and when; write "incident" or "event", not "breach", which is counsel's determination; label speculation as hypothesis (the [language card](/docs/escalation/communication/#language) has the wording). If counsel directs the investigation, follow their instructions on privilege and distribution.

<KeyTakeaway>
Regulatory frameworks are part of the boundary the investigation is conducted within, not paperwork after the fact. Scope is where the analyst recognizes which regimes may attach, commits the candidate awareness and discovery timestamps, preserves what the clocks will need, and pulls the owning teams into the case. Recognition and escalation. The interpretation belongs to privacy and legal.
</KeyTakeaway>
