# Two kinds of time

<div class="time-types">
  <div class="time-card">
    <h3>🕐 Historical review</h3>
    <p>Looking backward from the alert. How far back the investigation pulls evidence. Short windows (hours to a day) work for fast-moving attacks like ransomware staging. Longer windows (weeks to months) are needed for slow-burn insider cases or campaigns that establish persistence over time.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="time-card">
    <h3>⏱️ Real-time investigation</h3>
    <p>The window during which the investigation is happening now. Influences whether containment can happen mid-investigation and how quickly the handoff to incident response needs to be ready.</p>
  </div>
</div>

---

# Choosing the historical window

<div class="window-table">
  <div class="window-row">
    <span class="window-row__label">Ransomware staging</span>
    <span class="window-row__value">Last 1 to 24 hours</span>
    <p>Fast-moving. The attack typically progresses within a single shift, and the relevant evidence is recent.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="window-row">
    <span class="window-row__label">Phishing-driven intrusion</span>
    <span class="window-row__value">Last 24 to 72 hours</span>
    <p>Initial access happened recently; the question is what the adversary did with it after.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="window-row">
    <span class="window-row__label">Lateral movement</span>
    <span class="window-row__value">Last 1 to 4 weeks</span>
    <p>Adversaries dwell for days or weeks before moving. The historical baseline needs enough range to surface the gradual expansion.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="window-row">
    <span class="window-row__label">Supply-chain compromise</span>
    <span class="window-row__value">Last 1 to 6 months</span>
    <p>The compromise may predate the alert by weeks or months. Both the alert and earlier silent activity matter.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="window-row">
    <span class="window-row__label">Slow-burn insider</span>
    <span class="window-row__value">Last 3 to 12 months</span>
    <p>The trajectory is what reveals the case. A short window will not capture the gradual deviation.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout variant="info" title="Retention is a real constraint">
The window the analyst chooses is bounded by what the SOC actually retains. If logs are 30 days old and the case calls for a 6-month look, the investigation has to acknowledge that gap rather than pretend it does not exist. Document the retention limit as part of the Scope output.
</Callout>

---

# Real-time investigation considerations

- **Coverage model.** A 24/7 SOC and an 8/5 SOC handle real-time differently. After-hours alerts have different escalation paths.
- **Containment timing.** Some actions (isolating a host, disabling an account) can be taken during the investigation. Others have to wait for IR. Scope decides which is which based on the threat type and impact.
- **Time-boxing.** Open-ended investigations consume resources without producing results. Scope sets a soft deadline at which the investigation either escalates or closes, even if not every thread has been chased.

<KeyTakeaway>
Time is a boundary, not a default. The right window depends on the threat. Choosing deliberately and documenting why is what makes the result defensible and the work bounded.
</KeyTakeaway>
