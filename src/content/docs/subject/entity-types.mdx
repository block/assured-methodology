# The four entity types

Modern environments produce four broad categories of identities. Each one has its own normal patterns, its own ways of being abused, and its own analysis priorities.

<div class="entity-grid">
  <div class="entity-card entity-card--users">
    <span class="entity-card__tag">01</span>
    <h3>👤 User accounts</h3>
    <p>Standard users, administrators, service accounts, external partners. Identity verification and access monitoring drive the analysis.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="entity-card entity-card--endpoints">
    <span class="entity-card__tag">02</span>
    <h3>💻 Endpoints</h3>
    <p>Physical and virtual devices: workstations, servers, mobile, IoT, network infrastructure. The host is the address where activity actually happens.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="entity-card entity-card--apps">
    <span class="entity-card__tag">03</span>
    <h3>⚙️ Applications and services</h3>
    <p>Business applications, dev tools, automation pipelines, cloud functions. Non-human identities with predictable but consequential behavior.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="entity-card entity-card--network">
    <span class="entity-card__tag">04</span>
    <h3>🌐 Network identifiers</h3>
    <p>IPs, domains, protocols, MAC addresses, certificates. Not identities in the strict sense, but the markers the system uses to route trust.</p>
  </div>
</div>

---

# 👤 User accounts

User accounts produce the earliest and most actionable indicators of compromise. The analyst's job is to evaluate the activity against the account's expected pattern and lifecycle.

<div class="user-lifecycle">
  <div class="user-lifecycle__row">
    <h4>Creation and provisioning</h4>
    <p>Cross-reference account creation against HR onboarding and approved automation. Accounts that appear without a matching workflow are the classic backdoor pattern.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="user-lifecycle__row">
    <h4>Modification events</h4>
    <p>Privilege grants, group memberships, role changes. Each is a moment where an attacker could be establishing or expanding their access.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="user-lifecycle__row">
    <h4>Dormancy</h4>
    <p>Accounts inactive for 30+ days without being deprovisioned are a known soft target. They are credentials with no human watching them.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="user-lifecycle__row">
    <h4>Termination</h4>
    <p>Off-boarded users should lose access across every integrated system within hours of HR's record being updated. Orphaned credentials are how former employees become external attackers.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="user-lifecycle__row">
    <h4>Password resets</h4>
    <p>Resets outside the normal rotation policy, especially from unexpected locations, can indicate social engineering or account-recovery abuse.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## Subtypes of user accounts

<div class="user-subtypes">
  <div class="user-subtype">
    <h4>Standard users</h4>
    <p>Predictable patterns tied to business hours and job function. Most volume, least permission.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="user-subtype">
    <h4>Service accounts</h4>
    <p>Non-interactive by design. Highly predictable behavior. Interactive logins from a service account are a near-canonical compromise signal.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="user-subtype">
    <h4>Administrative accounts</h4>
    <p>High impact if abused. Should be used through privileged access management, not for routine work. Watch for admin accounts doing user-tier activities.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="user-subtype">
    <h4>External accounts</h4>
    <p>Contractors, partners, vendor logins. Limited organizational visibility, broader attack surface. Compensating controls (time-bound access, narrow scope) are essential.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="user-subtype">
    <h4>Shared accounts</h4>
    <p>Single credentials used by multiple people. Attribution becomes impossible. Treat any shared-account activity as a documentation risk before treating it as a security one.</p>
  </div>
</div>

---

# 💻 Endpoints

Each endpoint is a place where activity happens and a window into the broader environment.

<div class="endpoint-patterns">
  <div class="endpoint-card">
    <h4>Workstations</h4>
    <p>Standard user productivity machines. Compare current activity against the user's historical pattern on this specific device.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="endpoint-card">
    <h4>Servers</h4>
    <p>Mostly service-account driven, predictable workloads, narrow user-interactive activity. Interactive logins on production servers deserve a closer look.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="endpoint-card">
    <h4>Mobile devices</h4>
    <p>Personal and managed. MDM telemetry, MAM data, EMM enrollment status. Off-network activity may be invisible to corporate logging.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="endpoint-card">
    <h4>IoT and OT</h4>
    <p>Limited instrumentation. Often legacy. Trust boundaries depend on segmentation rather than endpoint detection.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="endpoint-card">
    <h4>Cloud workloads</h4>
    <p>EC2 instances, Kubernetes pods, serverless functions. Short lifetimes mean baselines are statistical, not per-instance.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="endpoint-card">
    <h4>Network infrastructure</h4>
    <p>Routers, switches, firewalls. Compromise here is rare but high impact. Configuration drift is the most common signal.</p>
  </div>
</div>

---

# ⚙️ Applications and services

Applications and services are non-human identities that act on behalf of (or alongside) human users.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why these often hide in plain sight">
Applications and services often have broad, persistent access and very predictable behavior. That predictability is what makes anomalies stand out. A CI/CD service account that suddenly accesses a database it has never touched before is a louder signal than the same access from a human, precisely because the service account's baseline is tight.
</Callout>

<div class="app-grid">
  <div class="app-card">
    <h4>Business applications</h4>
    <p>ERP, HR, finance, CRM. Sensitive data, predictable workflows, regulatory exposure.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="app-card">
    <h4>Development tools</h4>
    <p>Source control, CI/CD pipelines, package registries. Often have credentials that can deploy code into production.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="app-card">
    <h4>System utilities</h4>
    <p>Backup agents, monitoring tools, configuration management. Run continuously, touch many systems, easy to overlook.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="app-card">
    <h4>Cloud services</h4>
    <p>Managed cloud functions, container orchestration, service meshes. The identity boundary is often a cloud role rather than a credential.</p>
  </div>
</div>

---

# 🌐 Network identifiers

Network identifiers are how systems decide who is who when they cannot ask a person directly.

- **IP addresses.** Source and destination identities for any network traffic. Internal IPs map to assets via the asset inventory; external IPs map to reputation via threat intelligence.
- **Domain names.** Especially recent ones. Newly registered domains carry their own confidence weight.
- **Protocols and ports.** A workstation that suddenly speaks SMB to an unfamiliar host is a different story than one that speaks HTTPS.
- **MAC addresses.** Lower in the stack but useful for tracking devices across DHCP changes.
- **Certificates.** TLS fingerprints, code-signing certificates, client certs. Mismatches and generic CNs are signals.
- **API keys and tokens.** Persistent identifiers granted to services. Worth tracking by issuance and last-use date.

---

<KeyTakeaway>
Each entity type has its own pattern of normal and its own ways of being abused. Strong Subject analysis matches the analytical lens to the type. Treating a service account like a user (or a cloud role like a host) is how attackers go uninvestigated even when their activity is in the logs.
</KeyTakeaway>
