<ChapterExample
  title="Finding all the identities behind the alert"
  scenario="The Alert chapter handed Subject one named user (dlin, a finance analyst) and one host (laptop-finance-09) connected to a suspicious PowerShell event. Subject's job is to find every other identity that the alert touches and assess each one."
>

<ExampleStepper steps={["Start with the named user", "Expand to non-human identities", "Apply the four dimensions", "Check relationships", "Hand off to Scope"]}>

  <div data-step class="example-step">
    <h3>Step 1. Start with the named user</h3>
    <p>The alert names dlin. The Subject phase begins by profiling that user explicitly:</p>
    <ul class="example-step__list">
      <li><strong>Identity verification.</strong> The account is provisioned, matches an HR record, and is in the expected finance role.</li>
      <li><strong>Authentication history.</strong> Recent logins look normal until the day of the alert. Then a 09:08 login from the usual workstation, followed by the alert at 09:11.</li>
      <li><strong>Authorization.</strong> dlin has read access to finance reporting systems and the standard productivity stack. No administrative privileges.</li>
      <li><strong>Behavior.</strong> Activity profile is consistent with a finance analyst role. PowerShell is not part of dlin's normal pattern.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>Three of the four dimensions agree. The behavior dimension does not. PowerShell on this account is the first concrete signal that the alert is more than a misfire.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-step class="example-step">
    <h3>Step 2. Expand to non-human identities</h3>
    <p>Subject does not stop at the named user. Every identity touched by the activity gets profiled.</p>
    <ul class="example-step__list">
      <li><strong>The host.</strong> laptop-finance-09 is a managed corporate device, enrolled in the EDR, with a baseline of activity matching dlin's role.</li>
      <li><strong>The service account.</strong> Pre-attack, the laptop authenticated against a domain-joined identity that itself authenticates services on behalf of the user. The service account's recent activity becomes part of the picture.</li>
      <li><strong>The Outlook session.</strong> The parent process of the PowerShell invocation. Each Outlook session has its own token, mailbox identity, and audit trail.</li>
      <li><strong>The cloud identity.</strong> dlin's federated SSO identity for the cloud-based finance reporting platform. Has the same recent-login pattern as the on-premises account.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>One named user has become four identities. Each one is a potential lateral-movement vector. Each one needs its own assessment.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-step class="example-step">
    <h3>Step 3. Apply the four dimensions</h3>
    <p>The four dimensions are now applied to <em>every</em> identity in the list, not just dlin.</p>
    <ul class="example-step__list">
      <li><strong>Authentication.</strong> All four identities authenticated normally. No impossible travel, no MFA bypass, no token replay signals.</li>
      <li><strong>Authorization.</strong> No identity exceeded its assigned permissions during the relevant window. Authorization checks pass.</li>
      <li><strong>Behavior.</strong> dlin's user account behaved abnormally (PowerShell). The host's process baseline shows no prior PowerShell use under this user. The service account and cloud identity show no unusual activity.</li>
      <li><strong>Relationships.</strong> The four identities are tightly connected: same user, same device, same SSO chain. No surprising new connections to entities outside that cluster.</li>
    </ul>
    <p class="example-step__what-not"><strong>What a less experienced analyst could miss:</strong> applying the dimensions only to the named user. The service account and cloud identity are easy to overlook because the alert did not name them, but they share trust paths and could expose lateral movement if compromised.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-step class="example-step">
    <h3>Step 4. Check relationships</h3>
    <p>The relationship dimension expands beyond the four identities into the systems and people they touch.</p>
    <ul class="example-step__list">
      <li><strong>Communication graph.</strong> dlin's mailbox in the relevant window shows a recent email with an unfamiliar attachment from an external sender. The sender is a candidate for the originating phishing source.</li>
      <li><strong>Other recipients.</strong> The same email pattern landed in three other finance team mailboxes. Two of them opened the attachment. Two more potential compromise candidates.</li>
      <li><strong>Downstream access.</strong> The cloud finance platform that dlin can access is connected to a vendor payment workflow. If the compromise extends to the cloud identity, payment systems are in scope for the next phase.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>The Subject picture now includes four identities plus three potential additional victims plus a downstream payment workflow. That is the map.</p>
  </div>

  <div data-step class="example-step">
    <h3>Step 5. Hand off to Scope</h3>
    <p>Subject ends with a structured, defended view of every identity connected to the alert. The handoff to Scope answers:</p>
    <ul class="example-step__list">
      <li><strong>Primary entities.</strong> dlin (user), laptop-finance-09 (host), dlin's cloud identity (SSO).</li>
      <li><strong>Secondary entities.</strong> The service account dlin's laptop authenticates against. Two other finance users who opened the same email. The originating sender.</li>
      <li><strong>High-value paths.</strong> The cloud finance platform → vendor payment workflow. Lateral movement here would have regulatory and financial impact.</li>
      <li><strong>Insider risk indicators.</strong> None. The pattern looks like external phishing, not insider activity. No anti-forensic signs.</li>
    </ul>

    <Reveal summary="What the Subject deliverable looks like in practice" variant="example">
      <p>Subject's output is a short, structured document the next phase can act on without redoing the work:</p>
      <ul>
        <li>Map of every identity in scope (primary + secondary + downstream).</li>
        <li>Per-identity assessment across the four dimensions.</li>
        <li>List of relationships that expand the investigation beyond the alert.</li>
        <li>Recommended scope parameters: time window (24 hours before the alert), entities (the list above), depth of investigation (full for primary identities, baseline-comparison for secondary).</li>
      </ul>
      <p>Scope picks up here and turns this map into the formal boundary of the investigation.</p>
    </Reveal>
  </div>

</ExampleStepper>

</ChapterExample>

<KeyTakeaway>
The Subject phase turned one named user into a map of five-plus identities plus the downstream systems they touch. That expansion is the whole point. Without Subject, the investigation would have stayed on one user and one host. With Subject, it knows to follow the trust paths.
</KeyTakeaway>
