The subject analysis framework is a core component of the ASSURED methodology, providing security professionals with a structured approach to identify, evaluate, and monitor the digital entities interacting with an organization's systems. 
By examining four key dimensions  [authentication, authorization, behavior, and relationships] this framework empowers teams to build comprehensive profiles that enable accurate threat detection and mitigation.

While this overview introduces the purpose of each dimension, the following sections will explore them in greater detail. 
By examining authentication, authorization, behavior, and relationships individually, security teams gain the insight needed to distinguish between normal business activity and malicious intent, ultimately strengthening both detection and response capabilities.

# 1. Authentication: Verifying Identity Integrity
The authentication dimension of the subject analysis framework is a critical starting point for investigating potential security incidents. 
By closely examining the credentials and login patterns associated with each entity, security teams can detect and respond to identity-based threats like credential theft, account compromise, and unauthorized access attempts. 
The key to effective authentication-focused event triage is to quickly identify anomalies, validate identities, and thoroughly investigate the root cause of any suspicious activity.

Strong authentication practices form the foundation of organizational security. 
Weak or compromised authentication controls can allow attackers to masquerade as trusted users, bypassing defenses and escalating privileges undetected. 

> This dimension ensures that investigations start by asking a fundamental question: Is the entity really who they claim to be?

<br />

### Step 1: Detect Anomalies

The first step in authentication-based event triage is to analyze login data, geolocation, and device fingerprints to identify any suspicious patterns or outliers.

Common anomalies include:

<div class="grid grid-cols-1 md:grid-cols-2 gap-2 my-8">
  <div class="bg-slate-50 rounded-lg p-5">
    ##### Impossible Travel
    Impossible travel occurs when a user account authenticates from two geographically distant locations within a timeframe that makes physical travel between them impossible.
    This indicates potential credential compromise, use of proxy/VPN services, or malicious automation.
    > For example, a successful login from New York followed by another from Tokyo two minutes later suggests either stolen credentials or a misconfigured geolocation system.
    <br />
    ##### Key Analyst Considerations:
    - Is the account using a corporate VPN or known proxy that may explain location discrepancies?
    - Are there supporting signals such as MFA failures or abnormal device fingerprints?
    - Did the subsequent login trigger sensitive actions like privilege escalation?
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 rounded-lg p-5">
    ##### Velocity Anomalies
    Velocity anomalies detect unusually rapid sequences of successful authentication attempts by a single user or service, often indicative of brute-force attacks, scripted logins, or automated processes masquerading as human behavior.
    > The "velocity" refers to time-based frequency, such as a user successfully logging into five different systems in seconds—a pattern atypical for manual activity.
    ##### Key Analyst Considerations:
    - Compare against the user's baseline behavior (e.g., normal login cadence).
    - Determine whether the activity involves high-value systems or privileged accounts.
    - Look for corresponding failed attempts prior to the spike, suggesting credential stuffing or brute force.
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 rounded-lg p-5">
    ##### Unusual Networks
    Unusual networks represent authentication activity originating from networks not commonly associated with the account or organization, such as residential ISPs, foreign countries, anonymous VPNs, or Tor exit nodes.
    These often signal unauthorized access or attempts to obscure attacker identity.
    ##### Key Analyst Considerations:
    - Map IP addresses to known corporate ranges, partner networks, or approved vendor locations.
    - Check for presence on blocklists, known threat intelligence feeds, or anonymizing services.
    - Combine with device and geolocation data to determine if the access is legitimate remote work or malicious activity.
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 rounded-lg p-5">
    ##### Failed Login Spikes
    Failed login spikes occur when there is a sudden surge in failed authentication attempts, typically signaling brute-force activity, misconfigured systems, or an attacker testing credentials.
    While occasional failures are normal, significant clustering within a short window indicates a targeted attack or mass credential spray.
    ##### Key Analyst Considerations:
    - Identify whether failures come from a single source, multiple sources, or a distributed botnet.
    - Correlate with subsequent successful logins that may indicate compromise.
    - Determine if the spike aligns with known credential dumps or external breach events.
  </div>
</div>

> By leveraging IP geolocation, device fingerprinting, and threat intelligence feeds, security teams can quickly detect these types of anomalies and trigger alerts for further investigation. 
Analyzing login velocity and failed attempt data can also help identify potential brute force or password spraying attacks targeting user credentials.

<br />

### Step 2: Validate Identities

Once anomalous activity has been detected, the next step is to validate the legitimacy of the user accounts involved.

This involves:

<div class="grid grid-cols-1 md:grid-cols-3 gap-2 my-8">
  <div class="bg-slate-50 rounded-lg p-5">
    ##### Cross-Referencing HR Systems
    Cross-referencing authentication activity with Human Resources (HR) systems ensures the identity belongs to an active, legitimate employee or contractor.
    By comparing directory service data (e.g., Active Directory, IAM roles) with HR records, analysts can detect orphaned accounts, terminated employees still active, or privilege discrepancies.
    ##### Key Analyst Considerations:
    - Is the user still employed or recently offboarded?
    - Does the HR record match the directory metadata (name, department, manager)?
    - Are there multiple accounts linked to the same individual, indicating shadow identities?
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 rounded-lg p-5">
    ##### Role Alignment
    Role alignment verifies that a user’s access privileges match their current job responsibilities and are appropriate for their position.
    It focuses on least privilege enforcement and detecting privilege creep, where access accumulates over time without review.
    ##### Key Analyst Considerations:
    - Does the user's group membership match their HR-defined role or department?
    - Are there sensitive permissions (e.g., domain admin, production cloud access) that don’t fit the user's duties?
    - Was there a recent role change (promotion, transfer) that might explain new access?
    - Are there temporary privilege grants (e.g., Just-In-Time access) that were never revoked?
  </div>
  <div class="bg-slate-50 rounded-lg p-5">
    ##### Authentication Hardening
    Authentication hardening ensures that login mechanisms protecting the identity are robust and resistant to modern attack techniques.
    This focuses on enforcing strong authentication controls, identifying weaknesses, and correlating them with suspicious activity.
    ##### Key Analyst Considerations:
    - Is Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) enabled and enforced for the account?
    - Are there logins using legacy or insecure protocols (e.g., IMAP, NTLM, Basic Auth)?
    - Are password policies sufficient, and are there signs of shared credentials?
    - Are there token or session anomalies, such as refresh tokens used from unexpected locations?
  </div>
</div>

> By taking these steps to verify user identities, organizations can quickly rule out false positives and focus their investigation efforts on potential credential compromise or account misuse incidents.

# 2. Authorization: Validating Role Alignment and Privilege Boundaries

This dimension evaluates whether an entity's actions align with their defined role privileges and access rights. 
It requires mapping user and service account activity against a comprehensive entitlement matrix that accurately reflects job responsibilities, system boundaries, and least-privilege principles. 
By comparing observed behavior against established baselines, analysts can identify access misuse, policy violations, and privilege escalation attempts that might otherwise appear legitimate.

Effective authorization analysis relies on mapping user and service account activity against a centralized entitlement matrix, ensuring access accurately reflects job responsibilities, system boundaries, and least privilege principles. 
By comparing observed behavior with established baselines, analysts can identify policy violations, detect privilege escalation, and prevent unauthorized lateral movement before it evolves into a broader incident.

<br />
<br />

### Why Authorization Analysis Matters
Access violations are a frequent vector for both external compromise and internal abuse.
- Example 1: A contractor with legitimate access to a payment processing system attempted to deploy unauthorized code to extract transaction data. 
Their system presence was valid, but their behavior (modifying core financial logic) violated role boundaries and triggered containment protocols.

- Example 2: A junior IT administrator attempted to export sensitive patient data through an unapproved channel. 
Authorization analysis revealed the activity was outside their role scope, allowing the organization to act before sensitive information was exposed.

These examples highlight a key truth: <strong>legitimate access does not equal legitimate activity.</strong>

Authorization analysis provides the visibility needed to make that distinction.

<br />
<br />

### Permission Mapping
Permission mapping is the process of enumerating and visualizing all permissions granted to an identity, including direct assignments, group memberships, and inherited roles.
It establishes a clear picture of the account’s capabilities and highlights unexpected or dangerous entitlements.

#### Key Analyst Considerations:
- What groups, roles, or policies grant this user their permissions?
- Are there nested group memberships or inherited permissions creating hidden risk?
- Does the mapping match the principle of least privilege?
- Are there temporary or emergency access grants still lingering?

<br />

## Privilege Management
During triage and investigation, analysts must evaluate whether a user's actions exceed their assigned privilege level or deviate from established baselines for their role and system interactions.
Effective privilege management focuses on detecting abuse or exploitation before attackers achieve persistence or lateral movement.

### Focus Areas Include: 

**Privilege Escalation Attempts:**
Identify suspicious patterns such as users invoking administrative tools, modifying security policies, or exploiting misconfigurations to elevate their access.
    >Example: A non-admin account running commands associated with domain controller replication or registry modification.

**Excessive Grants:**
Detect users with permissions far beyond what is necessary for their role.
This often occurs due to legacy accounts, incomplete offboarding, or temporary permissions that were never revoked.

**Privilege Chaining:**
Spot when an attacker leverages multiple low-level privileges in combination to create a powerful, unauthorized capability.
    >Example: Combining read-only database access with write permissions on an API to extract sensitive data.

**Enforcing Least Privilege Through:**
- Routine access audits to continually validate role-to-permission alignment.
- Short-lived access tokens for elevated permissions, automatically expiring after a set window to minimize misuse opportunities.
- Clear escalation protocols for requesting and granting temporary administrative rights, with detailed tracking and justification.

<br />
<br />

## Access Control Integrity
This step evaluates the overall integrity of permission structures across the environment, focusing on how individual rights combine into risky or exploitable patterns.
Even well-intentioned role assignments can inadvertently create toxic permission pairings or broad access surfaces that violate compliance mandates and internal policies.

### Key Checks Include:
- Monitoring temporary or emergency access to ensure elevated rights are revoked promptly after use.
Emergency access should always generate audit events and be tied to incident tickets.
- Detecting role sprawl, where a user slowly accumulates permissions across unrelated systems or domains.
    >Example: A developer account also gaining finance system privileges over time without explicit approval.
- Identifying cross-functional access that enables malicious actions like data exfiltration, fraud, or operational sabotage.
    >Example: A user with both development permissions and unrestricted production deployment rights.

### Why This Matters:
Improper access integrity opens the door to both insider threats and external attackers, who exploit weak access control enforcement to hide their tracks and gain persistence.

<br />
<br />

## Administrative Oversight
Administrative and privileged accounts require the highest level of monitoring and control, as they have the potential to impact entire systems, workflows, and datasets.

### Analysts Should:
- Flag administrative activity that occurs outside approved maintenance windows or workflows.
Unexpected off-hours activity is a common indicator of compromise or insider abuse.
- Investigate shared accounts or service accounts performing interactive logins, as these obscure individual accountability and increase the difficulty of attribution.
- Require peer validation or secondary approvals for high-impact actions such as schema changes, production deployments, or large-scale data exports.
This reduces the risk of both intentional and accidental destructive actions.
- Monitor for attempts to bypass change management or approval processes, such as direct edits to production systems or manipulation of audit logs.
These activities often indicate malicious intent or policy violations.

# 3. Historical Behavior Analysis: Establishing Baselines for Anomaly Detection

Historical behavior analysis within the Subject phase focuses on determining whether current activity aligns with an entity's established behavioral norms. 
This involves creating a detailed behavioral profile through sustained observation—typically over a 90-day period or longer—that captures patterns such as access frequency to systems, peer group comparisons, and interaction habits with critical assets.

The baseline must account for role-specific expectations, team workflows, and seasonal fluctuations to reduce false positives caused by predictable organizational changes (e.g., quarterly reporting or end-of-year audit activity).

Once this baseline is established, deviations become valuable indicators during triage. 
Sudden changes, such as accessing sensitive systems outside normal hours, may point to compromised credentials or insider misuse. 
Gradual shifts, like a slow increase in access scope or exploratory system probing, can reveal long-term reconnaissance or staged escalation. 
Analysts must evaluate these anomalies in context—considering the user's role, privileges, and recent organizational changes—to distinguish legitimate evolution from potential threats. 
Tracking the persistence and trajectory of these deviations enables early detection of insider threats or account compromise before they escalate.

# 4. Relationship Mapping: Uncovering Hidden Connections and Lateral Movement

Relationship mapping within the Subject phase focuses on uncovering and analyzing the connections between users, systems, and data to reveal unusual interactions that may indicate compromise or misuse. 
By modeling these relationships as dynamic graphs, analysts can detect lateral movement, unauthorized access paths, and data exfiltration routes that traditional signature-based detections might miss.

The goal is to understand how entities normally interact, creating a relational baseline of communication and access patterns. 
When deviations occur (such as a user suddenly connecting to systems outside their usual scope or data moving through unexpected channels) analysts gain early warning of potential threats. 
This context-driven view supports faster triage and a more targeted response to complex security events.

Relationship mapping requires continuous monitoring of communication and activity across platforms like email systems, collaboration tools, endpoint telemetry, and identity systems. 
Over time, these connections form a living map of interactions, highlighting insider threats, compromised accounts, and hidden attack paths.
