## How the dimensions work together

The four dimensions are independent questions about the same <DefineTerm term="Entity" display="entity" />. Authentication asks how the entity got in. Authorization asks what the entity is allowed to do. Behavior asks what the entity is actually doing. Relationships ask what the entity is connected to.

When all four agree (authenticated normally, acted within its permissions, in line with its history, with relationships that match the role), the Subject assessment passes quickly. When even one disagrees, the disagreement is the lead.

---

## Authentication

The first dimension evaluates how the entity proved who it was. The job is not to assume the <DefineTerm term="Credential" display="credential" /> was used legitimately. The job is to evaluate the credentials themselves and the context they were used in.

<div class="dim-detail">
  <div class="dim-detail__step">
    <span class="dim-detail__num">01</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Detect anomalies</h3>
      <p>Compare login data, geolocation, and device fingerprints to historical norms. Impossible-travel patterns (a login from two cities 1,600 km (1,000 miles) apart within 30 minutes), brand-new devices, or sources from anonymizing services are first-pass signals.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="dim-detail__step">
    <span class="dim-detail__num">02</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Validate identity</h3>
      <p>Cross-reference the account against HR records, identity provider data, and role definitions. A login from an account with no matching HR or provisioning record (or one that was supposed to be off-boarded last week) is its own story. Asking the user is a contact with rules of its own: see <a href="/docs/escalation/communication/#verifying">Talking to people during triage</a>.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="dim-detail__step">
    <span class="dim-detail__num">03</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Investigate the session</h3>
      <p>Map the authentication path: source IP, intermediate identity providers, <DefineTerm term="Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)" display="MFA" /> outcome, session tokens issued. Look for unusually long sessions or the same <DefineTerm term="Token" display="token" /> presented from more than one location, the usual sign of a hijacked session. Anomalies in any of these reshape the assessment. Know what a reset does and does not end: a password reset alone does not reliably revoke refresh tokens, session cookies, or Kerberos tickets already issued. Access tokens stay valid until they expire (60 to 90 minutes by default in Entra ID) unless the resource enforces Continuous Access Evaluation. Refresh tokens last far longer (up to 90 days by default in Entra ID, renewed each time they are used), and a Kerberos ticket-granting ticket lasts 10 hours by default, renewable for up to 7 days. A compromised identity can still be live after its password changes, so revocation is explicit and verified.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

### Investigative techniques

Each technique below is a different question about the same authentication event.

<div class="technique-grid">
  <div class="technique-card">
    <span class="technique-card__num">01</span>
    <h4>Geolocation &amp; device analysis</h4>
    <p>Evaluate logins for access from unfamiliar locations or devices not typically associated with the user. Outliers in geography or device history often signal credential misuse. Impossible travel often arrives pre-scored by the identity provider (Microsoft Entra ID Protection's Atypical travel and Impossible travel, Okta's Velocity behavior), depending on licensing and configuration. Treat it as strong but not conclusive: VPN egress and in-flight Wi-Fi produce it too.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="technique-card">
    <span class="technique-card__num">02</span>
    <h4>Login patterns review</h4>
    <p>Investigate spikes in failed logins or rapid successions of attempts, especially when followed by a successful login. Brute force and <DefineTerm term="Password Spraying" display="password spraying" /> (many accounts hit with the same common <DefineTerm term="Password" display="password" />) often surface as many accounts each failing once or twice from the same source: Windows event 4625 (failed logon) on the target host, 4771 or 4776 on domain controllers, or Entra ID sign-in error 50126 and Okta failed-authentication events in the cloud. Per-account lockout thresholds miss the pattern, because no single account fails often.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="technique-card">
    <span class="technique-card__num">03</span>
    <h4>Network source inspection</h4>
    <p>Identify logins from anonymizers, Tor exit nodes, and known hostile infrastructure, but a clean residential IP proves nothing: attackers rent residential proxy networks precisely to blend in with home broadband (Midnight Blizzard did this against Microsoft). Frequent source-IP change is weak on its own, because mobile carriers, CGNAT, privacy relays such as iCloud Private Relay, and SASE/ZTNA egress all produce it. Corporate egress cuts the other way: behind a VPN concentrator or a forward proxy, every remote user appears to sign in from the same few company addresses, so a "known" IP proves little too. Weigh it alongside device and session continuity.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="technique-card">
    <span class="technique-card__num">04</span>
    <h4>MFA event auditing</h4>
    <p>Review authentication prompts for signs of excessive challenges, fallback method use, or repeated failures. These anomalies may indicate phishing, MFA fatigue, or bypass attempts. Number matching blunts plain push fatigue, so attackers now pair the prompts with a phone call that talks the user through approving, or with a help-desk reset.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="technique-card">
    <span class="technique-card__num">05</span>
    <h4>MFA method and device registration</h4>
    <p>Above all, check for MFA methods or devices registered or changed around the alert (the Entra ID audit activity "User registered security info", the Okta event user.mfa.factor.activate). Attackers enroll their own authenticator immediately after a phish or a help-desk reset, and the new method survives a password reset (T1098.005 Device Registration). A method the user did not ask for triggers the <a href="/docs/reference/identity-sweep/">identity-persistence sweep</a>, which checks the other anchors an attacker leaves behind.</p>
  </div>
</div>

</Callout>

### Cloud-identity patterns you'll see today

Exploited vulnerabilities are now the most common way in (31% of breaches in the 2026 Verizon DBIR), but credential and session abuse shows up somewhere in a large share of breaches (roughly four in ten, by industry summaries of the same report), and much of it runs through the identity provider. Subject does not need the protocol mechanics to triage these. It needs to recognize the pattern and know which entity the pattern adds to the map. The field-level depth (sign-in log anatomy, the device-code and session-replay signals, role-assumption chains) lives in [Cloud and identity logs](/docs/alert/parsing/cloud-identity/#idp-sign-in-logs).

| Pattern | What it adds to the Subject map | ATT&CK |
| --- | --- | --- |
| OAuth consent-grant abuse | An application identity with its own grant and refresh tokens, consented by the user or from a hijacked session. Password resets and MFA changes do not touch it (see [OAuth apps](/docs/subject/entity-types/#applications-and-services)). New secrets or certificates added to an existing app are the same pattern from the other side. | T1528, T1671; T1098.001 |
| <DefineTerm term="Adversary-in-the-Middle (AiTM)" display="AiTM" /> session theft | A session born in an interactive, MFA-satisfied sign-in from a hosting IP minutes after a link click, then replayed from somewhere else. Profile the session next to the account; the same proxy IP finds the kit's other victims. | T1539, then T1550.004 |
| <DefineTerm term="Device Code Phishing" display="Device-code phishing" /> | A device-code sign-in the user did not start, often followed by a new device registration. The user authenticates on the genuine page, so phishing-resistant MFA does not stop it; blocking the flow where it is not needed does. | T1528, then T1550.001 |
| Token replay | Non-interactive sign-ins or API calls on a token from an IP or client the user never authenticated from. | T1550.001, T1550.004 |
| Forged tokens and <DefineTerm term="Federation" display="federation" /> trust abuse | Tokens with no matching sign-in at the identity provider at all: minted from a stolen signing key (the Storm-0558 pattern) or through a federated domain or token-signing certificate an admin changed (Golden SAML). The identity provider's configuration becomes a subject in its own right. | T1606, T1606.002, T1484.002 |
| Kerberos ticket theft and <DefineTerm term="Kerberoasting" display="Kerberoasting" /> (on-premises) | A stolen ticket-granting ticket replayed with no password touched, or one account requesting service tickets (event 4769) for many accounts that carry a service principal name in a short window. AES tickets crack too, so the encryption type does not clear it. | T1550.003, T1558.003 |

Any user who reached the credential step of a phishing kit is credential-exposed until the password is reset, sessions are revoked, and MFA methods are reviewed.

The platform sources for an authentication question (Windows and AD security events, Entra ID and Okta sign-in logs, CloudTrail), with a starting Sentinel query, are in [Where to look, by platform](/docs/alert/parsing/cloud-identity/#where-to-look-by-platform). The protocol mechanics behind the patterns above, including Kerberos tickets and AD delegation, are in [Identity attacks at protocol depth](/docs/alert/parsing/cloud-identity/#identity-attacks-at-protocol-depth).

<Reveal summary="Example: token replay seen only in non-interactive sign-ins" variant="example">
Microsoft Entra ID Protection flags an anomalous token for kpatel. Her interactive sign-in log shows one clean sign-in at 07:58 from her managed laptop, MFA satisfied. Read alone, Authentication looks fine.

The non-interactive sign-in log tells a different story. From 10:41, refresh-token sign-ins for the same account reach Exchange Online and SharePoint from a hosting-provider ASN in another country, with a different device and no interactive sign-in from that address at all. The SessionId on those sign-ins matches the 07:58 session.

Every sign-in "succeeded", and the Authentication dimension still fails: the credentials passed, but the session is being used from somewhere the user never authenticated. The matching SessionId is what turns the hypothesis of a replayed token (T1550.001) into evidence.
</Reveal>

## Authorization

Authorization asks whether the action the entity took was permitted, and whether the permissions themselves are appropriate.

The dimension is easy to under-evaluate. An action that maps cleanly to the user's role looks fine in a glance. The deeper question is whether the role grants too much, or whether the role was modified recently to permit this exact action.

<div class="authz-grid">
  <div class="authz-card">
    <h3>Effective permissions</h3>
    <p>Look up what the entity can actually do: nested group memberships, inherited cloud policies, active PIM or just-in-time elevations. Compare that against the role's documented intent. (Building the <DefineTerm term="RBAC">Role-Based Access Control. A model where permissions are granted to roles rather than to individual users, and users inherit permissions by being assigned roles. RBAC makes access reviewable at the role level rather than per-user.</DefineTerm> matrix and running entitlement reviews is program work; use them if they exist.)</p>
  </div>
  <div class="authz-card">
    <h3>Privilege management</h3>
    <p>During triage, evaluate whether the entity's actions exceed its assigned privilege level. Look for elevation, excessive grants, or chained permissions that effectively make the role admin.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="authz-card">
    <h3>Recent entitlement changes</h3>
    <p>Check recent changes to the entity's roles, groups, or access levels. Unauthorized activity shortly after a promotion, a transfer, or off-boarding often reflects privilege misuse or insider risk, and a grant made minutes before the alerting action is a lead in its own right.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="authz-card">
    <h3>Toxic access pairings (segregation-of-duties conflicts)</h3>
    <p>Two permissions that are reasonable individually but dangerous together. The classic finance case: someone with both "approve invoice" and "modify vendor bank account." Each is normal in a role; the combination is fraud risk. Your GRC or identity governance (IGA) tool may already flag segregation-of-duties violations; check it before reasoning from scratch.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="authz-card">
    <h3>Administrative oversight</h3>
    <p>Flag administrative activity outside approved change windows. Treat a service account performing interactive actions as a likely compromise until proven otherwise (see <a href="/docs/subject/entity-types/">Entity types</a>). Shared-account use breaks policy and destroys attribution, so investigate it as both a documentation and a security problem.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout variant="info" title="Classifying access by sensitivity">
Authorization analysis works better when the organization classifies its data, and the systems that hold or move it, by sensitivity. Building that model is data-governance and security-program work, done long before any alert fires; the analyst's job is to know whether one exists and read the alert against it. A common pattern:

- **Restricted: crown jewels.** Customer data, financial records, source code for critical systems, secrets. Access is logged, peer-reviewed, and time-bound.
- **Confidential: sensitive operations.** Production <DefineTerm term="Infrastructure" display="infrastructure" />, payment workflows, employee data. Access requires explicit role assignment and is reviewed quarterly.
- **Internal: standard business.** Internal collaboration tools, project workspaces, day-to-day applications. Default access for the role.
- **Public: open.** Public-facing or non-sensitive information. Unrestricted: any authenticated employee can reach the internal portion, and the rest is public by design.

When an alert touches an entity's Restricted access, Authorization gets weighted differently than the same alert on Public access. Where no classification model exists, the analyst approximates the same question by hand for the specific access in front of them: how bad would this be if it were hostile?

Do not confuse these sensitivity classes with AD and Entra administrative tiering, where Tier 0 means the identity systems themselves (domain controllers, the identity provider, PKI). Any alert touching that tier is critical regardless of data classification.
</Callout>

| Platform | Sources and events |
| --- | --- |
| Windows and AD | Security log 4728, 4732, and 4756 (member added to a security-enabled global, local, or universal group); 4720 (account created) |
| Microsoft Entra ID | AuditLogs "Add member to role" and "Add member to group"; PIM role activations; "Consent to application" and app-role assignments for apps |
| AWS | CloudTrail AttachUserPolicy, AttachRolePolicy, PutRolePolicy, UpdateAssumeRolePolicy, CreateAccessKey |

</Reveal>

## Behavior

The behavior dimension compares the entity's overall conduct to its own history and its peers. Alert's validation already asked whether this one signal is normal for the environment and for the entity; Behavior widens the lens from the alerting event to everything this identity has been doing. This dimension is deep enough that it has its own sub-page. The [behavioral framework](/docs/subject/behavioral-framework/) covers the full workflow, from establishing baselines to peer-group comparison. The signals below are a quick pass worth running on every entity even when the full framework is not warranted; the framework page develops baselines and the anomaly families in depth.

<div class="dim-detail">
  <div class="dim-detail__step">
    <span class="dim-detail__num">01</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Baseline comparison</h3>
      <p>What does this entity normally do? When? With whom? Sudden access to unfamiliar sensitive systems, operating outside normal hours, or repeating tasks at unusual cadence are all baseline deviations worth investigating.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="dim-detail__step">
    <span class="dim-detail__num">02</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Privilege misuse</h3>
      <p>Misuse of permissions the entity legitimately holds: allowed, but the volume, target, or timing does not match the role's intent.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="dim-detail__step">
    <span class="dim-detail__num">03</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Access scope drift</h3>
      <p>Gradual expansion into new systems, tools, or data categories outside the entity's historical norm. Each step alone looks like onboarding. The trajectory is the signal.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="dim-detail__step">
    <span class="dim-detail__num">04</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Behavioral staging</h3>
      <p>Methodical but slow deviations: low-risk systems explored before high-value ones, file collection before exfiltration. Each step normalizes the next.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<Reveal summary="Where to look: Behavior" variant="deep-dive">

Start with whatever already holds the entity's history: a UEBA entity timeline or peer analytics if the SOC runs one (in Microsoft Sentinel, the BehaviorAnalytics table). Without one, build the comparison by hand and read the alert window against both the entity and its peers.

| Question | Sources |
| --- | --- |
| Does the alert window match the entity's own history? | UEBA entity timeline; otherwise a 30-day query of the entity's sign-ins, process launches, and data access |
| Do its peers do the same? | The same query run for two or three peers in the same role, team, or host class |
| Has its reach been creeping? | First-seen dates per application, host, repository, and data store over the past 30 to 90 days |
| Is it moving more data than usual? | Daily volumes from file and SaaS audit logs, DLP events, and mailbox audit (MailItemsAccessed in Microsoft 365) |

</Reveal>

## Relationships

The relationships dimension maps the connections between the entity and other identities, systems, and data. Compromise rarely lives in one identity. It lives in the graph the identity participates in.

<Callout variant="info" title="What relationships look like in telemetry">
- **Communication patterns:** who this entity emails, messages, or shares documents with.
- **Authentication graph:** which systems this entity logs into, and which other entities log into the same systems.
- **SSO movement:** which additional applications this identity reached through federated sign-in during the window. A burst of first-time SSO app access after a suspicious sign-in is lateral movement across federated identities.
- **Access dependencies:** which downstream services this entity's actions affect (a developer who deploys → which production systems get changes).
- **Trust relationships:** federated identity providers, roles the entity can assume (AWS IAM), service accounts it can impersonate (GCP), service principals or app registrations it owns or holds credentials for (Entra ID), and API keys it owns. Read the trust in both directions: [who can become this identity](/docs/subject/non-human-identities/#who-can-become-this-identity) matters as much as what it can become. In Active Directory, delegation is a trust path too: a service configured for unconstrained delegation holds the tickets of everyone who authenticates to it, so "web server compromised" can mean "every admin who signed in to it while their ticket is still valid is captured too."
</Callout>

### Relationship mapping steps

<DefineTerm term="Relationship Mapping" display="Relationship mapping" /> is its own small methodology, and its steps split across two altitudes. Step 01 is platform work: the connection graph is assembled by the identity and analytics layer (the IdP, the SIEM, UEBA tooling) before any alert fires, and no analyst builds it per-case. Steps 02 through 04 are the triage moves, run as queries against that pre-built graph for the entity in question. Knowing which altitude you are at keeps a junior from reading step 01 as their job at minute 12.

<div class="rel-steps">
  <div class="rel-step">
    <span class="rel-step__num">01</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Analyze connections <span class="rel-step__tag">program layer</span></h4>
      <p>The environment maintains a map of relationships between users, systems, and data, built by linking identities and entities across telemetry. This baseline captures normal communication and access patterns. Attack-path tools (BloodHound CE for AD and Entra ID, Cartography or PMapper for AWS, Defender for Identity lateral movement paths) precompute this graph; if your team runs one, query the entity's outbound paths before building anything by hand. At triage, the analyst pulls this entity's slice of the graph, or approximates one from IdP and access logs when no platform maintains it.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="rel-step">
    <span class="rel-step__num">02</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Identify deviations</h4>
      <p>Compare current interactions against peer groups and typical workflows to find unauthorized or unusual access attempts. Ask: has this entity touched this system before, and do its peers?</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="rel-step">
    <span class="rel-step__num">03</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Measure changes</h4>
      <p>Query this entity's interaction volume and counterparties over the past 30 days and compare the alert window against it: a jump in new counterparties or data volume is the signal, and may indicate reconnaissance, insider activity, or staging for an attack.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="rel-step">
    <span class="rel-step__num">04</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Analyze sequences</h4>
      <p>Study the timing and order of activities to uncover coordinated actions signaling lateral movement or staged attacks. Recognizing these temporal patterns helps connect isolated alerts into a wider incident.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<Reveal summary="Where to look: Relationships" variant="deep-dive">

| Question | Sources |
| --- | --- |
| What can this identity reach? | The attack-path graph if one exists; group and role memberships; AWS trust policies; Entra ID app ownership and credentials |
| Where did it go through SSO? | IdP sign-in logs grouped by application, first-seen per app |
| Which hosts did it log on to? | Windows 4624 logon type 3 and 10 on the destination hosts; 4648 on the source host |
| Which roles did it assume? | CloudTrail AssumeRole chains (sourceIdentity, role session name); Entra ID AADServicePrincipalSignInLogs and AADManagedIdentitySignInLogs |

</Reveal>

<Reveal summary="Example: how relationships expose lateral movement" variant="example">
A finance team user, dlin, signs in on their laptop at 09:14. Normal. The laptop then authenticates against a <DefineTerm term="Domain Controller" display="domain controller" /> at 09:16. Normal for a typical workday. At 09:18, a developer's workstation logs event 4624, logon type 3, with the finance laptop as the source and dlin as the account (or, worse, the finance laptop logs 4648, an explicit-credential logon with an admin account). That is the signal. The finance user's identity touched a workstation that the user has never interacted with before, and that workstation is in a different department.

Single-entity analysis would have closed this at the laptop. Relationship mapping is what extends the question into "what else does this identity now reach?"
</Reveal>

<KeyTakeaway>
The four dimensions are how an entity becomes a profile. Most Subject failures come from skipping a dimension. The strongest Subject assessments work through all four explicitly, even when one of them is obviously fine, because the act of confirming "fine" is the documentation that defends the verdict later.
</KeyTakeaway>
