  Subject is the <em>who</em> of the alert. Every entity that authenticated, executed, or acted on the systems the alert touched. Not just humans. Anyone or anything with an identity that the system trusted.
</ChapterHero>

<Callout variant="info" title="What Subject is for">
Alert told the analyst what fired. Subject answers <em>who acted</em>. The answer is rarely a single person. It is often a chain of identities (a user, the laptop they were on, a service account the laptop authenticated against, a cloud role the service account assumed) and the relationships between them. Subject is how the investigation moves from event to actor.
</Callout>

<Callout variant="info" title="How the sub-pages fit together">
The sub-pages aren't equivalent things to memorize. They're <em>different shapes</em> of the same question. **[Four dimensions](/docs/subject/four-dimensions/)** is the *primary lens*: apply it to every entity (Authentication, Authorization, Behavior, Relationships) to turn it into a structured profile. **[Entity types](/docs/subject/entity-types/)** is the *typology* of what to apply it to (user accounts, endpoints, applications and services, network identifiers), with the first checks for each. **[Non-human identities](/docs/subject/non-human-identities/)** takes the identities no person sits behind (service accounts, cloud roles, workload identities, OAuth apps, CI/CD tokens, AI agents) and shows how to find the owner and the baseline for each. **[Behavioral framework](/docs/subject/behavioral-framework/)** is the *toolkit* for the Behavior dimension: pattern recognition, baselines, anomalies. **[Insider analysis](/docs/subject/insider-analysis/)** is the *special case*: what to do differently when the actor turns out to be an authorized user, framed against the Insider Threat Matrix. Read in that order and the chapter feels like one continuous skill, not a set of disconnected sub-skills.
</Callout>

## Subject at a glance

<PhaseAtAGlance phase="subject">
  <Fragment slot="question-detail">Every identity that authenticated, executed, or acted, human or not, and how the identities connect.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="deliverable">The Subject handoff: the entity map, baselines and deviations with their confidence, the relationship graph, and insider indicators, carried in the handoff document the <a href="/docs/subject/transition/">transition to Scope</a> lays out.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="done"><ul><li>every identifier resolves to an entity with an owner, and the confidence of each hop is noted;</li><li>every entity in the chain has a line per dimension (fine, not fine, or unknown), labeled per the <a href="/docs/documentation/standards/#confidence">shared confidence rubric</a>;</li><li>what could not be checked is written down as an open question for Uncover;</li><li>first-pass scope parameters are recommended.</li></ul></Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="tripwire">An escalation criterion confirmed during Subject (supported at High confidence) escalates now under the <a href="/docs/escalation/criteria/">escalation tripwire</a>. Subject keeps working as scoping support under IR's clock. Suspicion refines Scope instead.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="level-0">Subject compresses into one item of the <a href="/docs/fast-path/">eligibility gate</a>: not a privileged identity, and not automation holding deploy, signing, secrets, or repository-write rights, each proven from its source system. Unknown fails the gate. A privileged identity always fails it and routes the case to Level 2.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="level-1">Subject and Scope run as one context sweep: <em>who is this, and what can they reach?</em> The ten-minute pass below is the Subject half. A second subject, a privileged account, or regulated data the activity plausibly touched moves the case to Level 2.</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="level-2">The full chapter: every identity in the chain, human and non-human, profiled across the four dimensions, baselines read at every available scale, the insider check, and the complete handoff.</Fragment>
</PhaseAtAGlance>

<Callout variant="info" title="Subject in ten minutes">
In a full Level 2 investigation an experienced analyst typically spends 8 to 15 minutes on Subject and a new analyst 20 to 40: a diagnostic range, not a quota (see the [time-per-phase table](/docs/fast-path/#time-per-phase-by-experience-level)). The rest of the chapter describes far more work than that, so this is what "done enough" looks like on a routine case. Every step links to the page that develops it.

1. **Resolve the identifier to an owner** and note the confidence of each hop ([From identifier to identity](/docs/subject/entity-types/#from-identifier-to-identity)).
2. **List the non-human identities in the chain:** service accounts, cloud roles, OAuth apps, AI agents ([Entity types](/docs/subject/entity-types/), [Non-human identities](/docs/subject/non-human-identities/)).
3. **Write one line per dimension:** fine, not fine, or unknown ([Four dimensions](/docs/subject/four-dimensions/)).
4. **Check the baseline at the highest available scale:** the entity's own history, then its peers, then its role's expectations ([Behavioral framework](/docs/subject/behavioral-framework/#baseline-development)).
5. **Record what could not be checked.** A gap is an open question, not a clean result.

When any of the five turns up something that does not fit, that is the lead, and the deeper pages are where to follow it.
</Callout>

## Subjects are not just users

A common mistake in Subject analysis is to map "<DefineTerm term="Entity" display="entity" />" to "<DefineTerm term="User Account" display="user account" />." Modern environments have far more identities than people. Understanding what counts as a subject is the first skill of the phase. The four types below are the same four the [Entity types](/docs/subject/entity-types/) page develops.

<div class="evasion-grid">
  <div class="evasion-card evasion-card--subject">
    <h3>User accounts</h3>
    <p>Standard users, administrators, contractors, vendor logins, federated and guest identities, and the service accounts that sign in like them. Service accounts are non-human identities: they often hold broader access than people and outlive the people who created them.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="evasion-card evasion-card--subject">
    <h3>Endpoints</h3>
    <p>Workstations, servers, mobile devices, VMs (EC2, Azure VMs, GCE), Kubernetes pods. The host is the place where activity actually happens, and each one is an identity boundary the SOC needs to track.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="evasion-card evasion-card--subject">
    <h3>Applications and services</h3>
    <p>Deployment pipelines, automation bots, serverless functions, OAuth apps, AI agents, and the cloud roles and managed identities they run as. Non-human identities with predictable but consequential behavior.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="evasion-card evasion-card--subject">
    <h3>Network identifiers</h3>
    <p>IP addresses, MAC addresses, domains. Not identities themselves, but the markers systems use to decide who is who. Certificates and API keys are credentials that belong to the identities above.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## Why Subject deserves its own phase

A pattern experienced analysts will recognize: many missed intrusions are missed at Subject. The detection engine fired correctly and the alert was understood, but the analyst stopped at "<DefineTerm term="User" display="user" /> X did Y" without asking what kind of identity X is, what X normally does, or what other identities connect to X.

<div class="evasion-grid">
  <div class="evasion-card evasion-card--subject">
    <h3>Identity is not the same as person</h3>
    <p>The user account named alice@example.com is an identity. So is the service account that alice's CI/CD pipeline uses. So is the cloud role that pipeline assumes when it deploys to staging. Each can hold more trust than alice herself: if alice can trigger the pipeline, she effectively wields the pipeline's role. Collapsing all three into "alice" is how <DefineTerm term="Privilege Escalation" display="privilege escalation" /> paths get missed.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="evasion-card evasion-card--subject">
    <h3>Identities outlive people</h3>
    <p>Service accounts created five years ago, by engineers who have since left, often still have production access. They have no manager, no review cadence, and no normal user to compare against. Subject analysis is what surfaces these forgotten identities.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="evasion-card evasion-card--subject">
    <h3>Behavior is the strongest signal</h3>
    <p>For any identity, the question "is this normal for this identity" is more informative than "is this normal in general." A 03:00 login is unremarkable for a night-shift on-call engineer and alarming for a finance executive based in the same office. The same alert means different things depending on whose history it touches.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="evasion-card evasion-card--subject">
    <h3>Relationships reveal scope</h3>
    <p>A compromised identity is rarely the destination. It is the starting point for <DefineTerm term="Lateral Movement" display="lateral movement" />. Mapping which other identities and systems a compromised entity can reach is how the analyst sets the bounds of the investigation, which feeds directly into the Scope phase next.</p>
  </div>
</div>
