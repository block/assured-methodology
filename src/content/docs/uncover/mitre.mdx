# The three levels

<div class="mitre-grid">
  <div class="mitre-card">
    <span class="mitre-card__tag">Tactics</span>
    <h3>The why</h3>
    <p>What the adversary is trying to accomplish at a given step: initial access, persistence, privilege escalation, defense evasion, credential access, discovery, lateral movement, collection, exfiltration, impact. Tactics are the categories.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="mitre-card">
    <span class="mitre-card__tag">Techniques</span>
    <h3>The how</h3>
    <p>The specific method the adversary used. "T1566" is the technique for phishing. "T1059.001" is PowerShell as a command and scripting interpreter. Techniques are granular enough to map to detection rules and to teach analysts what to look for.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="mitre-card">
    <span class="mitre-card__tag">Procedures</span>
    <h3>The exact thing</h3>
    <p>The actual implementation of a technique as observed in the wild. "PowerShell Empire's <code>-EncodedCommand</code> download cradle" is a procedure. Procedures change rapidly; techniques less so.</p>
  </div>
</div>

# How an analyst uses ATT&CK

- **During Uncover.** Map each observed behavior to its ATT&CK technique. The chain of techniques becomes the investigation's narrative.
- **For hand-off.** Naming techniques lets the IR team start work from a shared model rather than re-reading the analyst's notes.
- **For detection engineering.** When a technique was observed but not detected automatically, that gap is the feature request for a new rule.
- **For trend analysis.** Tagging cases with techniques over time shows what the SOC sees most often, which feeds prioritization.

<KeyTakeaway>
ATT&CK is the vocabulary that makes Uncover findings portable. The chain of techniques is the artifact that survives the analyst and the alert; "T1566 → T1059.001 → T1071" travels better than free-form prose.
</KeyTakeaway>
