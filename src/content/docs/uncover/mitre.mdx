### The four levels

<div class="mitre-grid">
  <div class="mitre-card">
    <span class="mitre-card__tag">Tactics</span>
    <h3>The why</h3>
    <p>The adversary's tactical goal: the reason for performing an action. Categories representing the "why" behind each step of an attack. Things like Initial Access, Persistence, Stealth, Impact.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="mitre-card">
    <span class="mitre-card__tag">Techniques</span>
    <h3>The how</h3>
    <p>The specific method used to accomplish a tactic. Each technique has a unique ID (e.g., T1566 for Phishing). Granular enough to map to detection rules and to teach analysts what to look for.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="mitre-card">
    <span class="mitre-card__tag">Sub-techniques</span>
    <h3>The how, narrower</h3>
    <p>Granular variations of a parent technique with distinct characteristics. T1566 (Phishing) has sub-techniques T1566.001 (Attachment), T1566.002 (Link), T1566.003 (via Service), T1566.004 (Voice).</p>
  </div>
  <div class="mitre-card">
    <span class="mitre-card__tag">Procedures</span>
    <h3>The exact thing</h3>
    <p>Documented examples of techniques as implemented by specific threat actors in observed attacks. The granular operational steps that distinguish one actor from another.</p>
  </div>
</div>

ATT&CK also catalogs groups, software, campaigns, mitigations and detection strategies, all linked from each technique page.

ATT&CK is built from documented incidents, not theoretical attack models: MITRE curates it from public threat reporting and community contributions. New techniques arrive in the major releases each April and October. Since v19.2 (August 2026), smaller agile releases add Groups, Software, and Campaigns between them. Expect lag: when a behavior has no good fit, describe it in prose rather than forcing a mapping.

<Callout variant="info" title="Looking up a technique by ID">
Every ATT&CK identifier resolves to a canonical page at <a href="https://attack.mitre.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">attack.mitre.org</a>. The URL pattern is predictable: <code>https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1059/</code> for a top-level technique, <code>https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/001/</code> for a sub-technique, and <code>https://attack.mitre.org/tactics/TA0001/</code> for a tactic. When this site cites an ID, you can paste it into that URL pattern to reach the authoritative description, detection guidance, and known procedures. Since v18 (October 2025) each technique links to Detection Strategies (DETxxxx) and Analytics (ANxxxx), which name the data components and log sources that can show the behavior. Use them to turn a suspected technique into a query against the right source from the <a href="/docs/uncover/data-sources/">data-sources pillar</a>.
</Callout>

## The 15 tactics

Enterprise ATT&CK defines 15 tactics: the tactical goals an adversary pursues during an intrusion. The thirteen below are the ones you can observe in your own telemetry; the callout after the grid covers the two pre-intrusion tactics.

Adversaries rarely progress linearly through them, and they may revisit a tactic several times during a single campaign. The tactic tells you what the adversary was trying to achieve at that step; the technique is what maps to specific mitigations and detection strategies. When a technique serves several tactics (T1078 Valid Accounts serves four), record the tactic that matches the adversary's purpose at that point in the timeline.

One recent change worth knowing when you read older material: v19 (April 2026) split the long-standing Defense Evasion tactic into <strong>Stealth</strong> (hiding and blending in) and <strong>Defense Impairment</strong> (breaking the defenses themselves). Reports and detection rules written before then still say "Defense Evasion"; MITRE publishes v18-to-v19 crosswalk files for remapping them. The same churn is why every mapping should record the ATT&CK version it was made against.

<Callout variant="info" title="Two pre-intrusion tactics rarely observable from inside">
ATT&CK also defines <strong>TA0043 Reconnaissance</strong> (attacker information-gathering before initial access) and <strong>TA0042 Resource Development</strong> (infrastructure and capability setup: domain registration, malware compilation, account creation). Both mostly happen before the attacker touches your environment, so you will rarely catch them during triage. They show up as consequences (the registered domain that resolves to a C2 IP, the spear-phish that lands in a mailbox), with two exceptions: active scanning (T1595) hits your perimeter and WAF logs, and lookalike-domain registrations (T1583.001) can surface through certificate-transparency and brand monitoring. Read them when you're consuming a threat-intel report; don't expect to detect them on shift.
</Callout>

## Procedures: how specific actors implement techniques

Procedures represent the specific implementations of techniques used by threat actors or <DefineTerm term="Malware" display="malware" /> families. While multiple adversaries may employ identical techniques, their procedural implementations often differ significantly based on sophistication, resources, and intent.

Each card below is a procedure entry from the actor's ATT&CK group page, with the actor's names in other vendors' reporting and a link to the source.

<Reveal summary="Why procedural variation matters" variant="info">

Two actors might both leverage PowerShell for <DefineTerm term="Execution" display="execution" /> (T1059.001) but with very different procedural choices.

One actor might deploy heavily obfuscated scripts with encrypted payloads, prioritizing stealth at the cost of speed. Another might use simpler one-liners that finish quickly but show plainly in logs.

Procedural patterns are what link an investigation to a known <DefineTerm term="Threat Actor" display="threat actor" /> when multiple groups use the same technique. They are also what tell defenders which detection rule is going to fire and which is going to miss.

The levels change at different speeds. Tactics are stable for years, techniques change slowly, and procedures shift with every tooling update. That gradient is the ATT&CK view of the <a href="/docs/uncover/threat-intelligence/#ttp-hunting">Pyramid of Pain</a>.

</Reveal>

## Mapping a finding, step by step

Mapping is the ATT&CK skill a triage analyst uses every day. Start from the behavior in the evidence, not from the tool name or the alert title. One event from a typical phishing chain shows the method:

1. **Describe the behavior from the raw event.** <code>winword.exe</code> spawns <code>powershell.exe -enc ...</code> on a user's workstation: scripted execution, launched from an Office document.
2. **Name the adversary's purpose at that step.** Running code: the tactic is Execution.
3. **Pick the most specific technique the evidence supports.** PowerShell is a named sub-technique, so T1059.001, not the parent T1059.
4. **Label each mapping's confidence on the <a href="/docs/documentation/standards/#confidence">shared rubric</a>, and say what it rests on.** T1059.001 is HIGH (direct process evidence on the host). The delivery, T1566.001 Spearphishing Attachment, is MEDIUM: the email log shows the document arriving as an attachment, but the host never showed the user opening the message.
5. **Record the ATT&CK version** you mapped against (for example, "Enterprise v19").

<Callout variant="warn" title="Three common mapping errors">
<strong>Mapping the tool instead of the behavior:</strong> Cobalt Strike is software (S0154); what it did on this host is the technique. <strong>Tactic-only mapping:</strong> "Execution" says what the adversary wanted, not how, and maps to no detection. <strong>Over-mapping:</strong> tagging every technique a tool could perform instead of the ones the evidence shows. CISA's <a href="https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/best-practices-mitre-attckr-mapping" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Best Practices for MITRE ATT&CK Mapping</a> walks through the method in more depth, and its open-source <a href="https://github.com/cisagov/Decider" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Decider</a> tool guides an analyst to a technique through a question tree.
</Callout>

<Reveal summary="Related MITRE and community resources" variant="info">

- **<a href="https://mitre-attack.github.io/attack-navigator/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ATT&CK Navigator</a>:** layers over the matrix for a case's techniques, a quarter's trend, or detection coverage.
- **<a href="https://center-for-threat-informed-defense.github.io/attack-flow/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Attack Flow</a>:** the Center for Threat-Informed Defense's format for chaining techniques into a sequence. ATT&CK's tactics are deliberately unordered; Attack Flow is how the chain gets its order.
- **Kill-chain models:** Lockheed Martin's Cyber Kill Chain (2011) and Paul Pols' <DefineTerm term="Unified Kill Chain" /> (2017: In, Through, Out) give a reconstructed chain an ordered frame when the record has to say how far an intrusion got. ATT&CK supplies the technique names inside each stage.
- **<a href="https://d3fend.mitre.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">D3FEND</a>:** a knowledge graph of defensive countermeasures, linked to the offensive techniques they address.
- **<a href="https://atlas.mitre.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ATLAS</a>:** adversary tactics and techniques against AI-enabled systems, for cases where an AI assistant or model is the target.
- **<a href="https://car.mitre.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CAR</a>:** MITRE's Cyber Analytics Repository of detection analytics mapped to techniques.
- **Sigma rules:** most public Sigma rules carry ATT&CK tags, so a rule that fired often names its technique for you. Check the tag against the evidence before copying it into the record.

</Reveal>

## How ATT&CK is used, from the case to the program

<div class="usage-grid">
  <div class="usage-card">
    <h3>During Uncover</h3>
    <p>Map each observed behavior to its ATT&CK technique. The chain of techniques becomes the investigation's narrative spine.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="usage-card">
    <h3>For handoff</h3>
    <p>Naming techniques lets the IR team start work from a shared model rather than re-reading the analyst's notes.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="usage-card">
    <h3>For detection engineering</h3>
    <p>When a technique was observed but not detected automatically, that gap is <a href="/docs/detection-feedback/">the feature request for a new rule</a>: a new-detection request with the technique and its Detection Strategy (DET ID) attached.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="usage-card">
    <h3>For trend analysis</h3>
    <p>Tagging cases with techniques over time shows what the SOC sees most often, which feeds prioritization. Record the ATT&CK version with each mapping (e.g., "ATT&CK v19"). IDs get merged, revoked and re-parented between releases, and MITRE publishes crosswalk files to remap older tags.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="usage-card">
    <h3>For executive communication</h3>
    <p>Tactic names (not IDs) give leaders a stable frame for "how far did they get"; the technique IDs stay in the appendix.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="usage-card">
    <h3>For gap analysis</h3>
    <p>Mapping defensive coverage against the matrix surfaces where detection is strong and where it is thin. A colored cell means at least one analytic maps to the technique, not that its procedures are covered. Since v18 (October 2025), each technique carries a Detection Strategy (DET ID) with platform-specific Analytics (AN IDs) built on Data Components; measure coverage against those. Drives investment decisions.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<KeyTakeaway>
ATT&CK is the vocabulary that makes Uncover findings portable. The chain of techniques is the artifact that survives the analyst and the alert; "T1566.001 → T1059.001 → T1071.001" travels better than free-form prose. It travels into IR, detection engineering and threat-trend reporting, and translates into plain tactic names for executive briefings.
</KeyTakeaway>
