## Five ways to use intel

<div class="ti-grid">
  <a href="#indicator-matching" class="ti-card"><h3>Indicator matching</h3><p>The simplest use. Compare observed artifacts against known-bad lists. Reactive by nature.</p></a>
  <a href="#enrichment" class="ti-card"><h3>Automated enrichment</h3><p>Append intel data to alerts in flight. Reduces manual lookup work and speeds triage.</p></a>
  <a href="#attribution" class="ti-card"><h3>Adversary attribution</h3><p>Connect observed behavior to specific actors or campaigns. Useful for context, less directly actionable.</p></a>
  <a href="#ttp-hunting" class="ti-card"><h3>TTP-based hunting</h3><p>Use known techniques to search for similar patterns. More durable than indicator matching.</p></a>
  <a href="#dark-web" class="ti-card"><h3>Dark-web monitoring</h3><p>Watch for leaked credentials, organization mentions, attack planning. Early warning signal.</p></a>
</div>

<DefineTerm term="Threat Intelligence" display="Threat intelligence" /> works as a correlation layer across your own data (logs, EDR <DefineTerm term="Telemetry" display="telemetry" />, network traffic), not in isolation. During Uncover it helps validate, prioritize and contextualize what the evidence shows.

The page starts with the two things a triage analyst does on a live case: validating an intel match and reading enrichment. Attribution follows. The program machinery that produces the matches (the indicator pipeline, hunting, dark-web collection, intelligence tiers) comes last.

## An IOC hit on your case

A proxy alert fires: a workstation connected to <code>update-telemetry.example</code>, which a commercial feed lists as "C2, high confidence". The match is the question, not the answer. Working it takes a few minutes:

1. **Read the match itself.** Which feed, which tag, when the feed first listed the domain, which IP it reported, and the TLP marking on the report.
2. **Check your own history.** Was the domain seen in your environment before the feed listed it, and by how many hosts? One host, first seen today, is a different case from a domain 300 hosts have resolved daily for a year.
3. **Check what it resolved to at the time.** Passive DNS for the moment of your connection: is it the IP the feed reported, and is that IP shared hosting or a CDN edge, where an IP proves little?
4. **Look it up, do not touch it.** Reputation and relations from passive sources (GreyNoise, VirusTotal relations, urlscan.io history). No uploads, no browsing to the domain from the corporate network; the <a href="#enrichment">enrichment OPSEC rules</a> below explain why.
5. **Go back to your own telemetry.** Which process made the connection, what preceded it on the host, and whether anything was downloaded or executed.
6. **Label it on the shared scale.** For example: "Validated, MEDIUM: resolved to the reported C2 IP at connection time; an unsigned process made the call; no second independent source yet." Or: "Not supported: the reported IP is a shared CDN edge, the domain has been resolved fleet-wide since last year, and the caller is the browser loading a vendor's assets." The second is a false positive (data) for that feed entry, and the case record says so, which is the feedback step 06 of the indicator pipeline depends on.

## Validation and confidence

Not all intel is equal. Before acting on a piece of intelligence, validate it across four checks.

<div class="validate-grid">
  <div class="validate-card">
    <h3>Source reliability</h3>
    <p>Where did this come from? Typically higher-reliability sources include government agencies, established vendors and vetted ISACs, but reliability is a track record, not a category. Grade each source on its history; the Admiralty (NATO) system's A-F source reliability and 1-6 information credibility is the standard shorthand.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="validate-card">
    <h3>Recency</h3>
    <p>When was this observed? Indicators decay at different rates: IPs in shared cloud and residential-proxy ranges can be reassigned to legitimate tenants within days, domains get re-registered, and hashes stay malicious but grow less relevant as tooling changes. Retire indicators by type, not on a single clock.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="validate-card">
    <h3>Specificity</h3>
    <p>Is the intel about the exact thing being investigated, or a loose analogy? "This domain is associated with phishing" is less actionable than "This specific subdomain pattern is used by this specific campaign."</p>
  </div>
  <div class="validate-card">
    <h3>Corroboration</h3>
    <p>Does any other source say the same thing? Two independent sources reporting the same indicator is a stronger signal than one source repeating itself. Check that the sources really are independent: five feeds republishing one blog post are one source.</p>
  </div>
</div>

### One scale, not two

Validated intel is labeled with the same LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH scale used for <a href="#attribution">attribution</a> below and defined once in the <a href="/docs/documentation/standards/#confidence">shared rubric</a>: the four checks map directly onto it (corroboration and source reliability drive the level; recency and specificity cap it). The only state this section adds is **unverified**: intel that has not yet been scored against the four checks. Unverified intel can generate hypotheses, but it never drives an operational decision until it has been validated and labeled.

<Callout variant="info" title="Intel is a hypothesis, not a verdict">
Threat intelligence tells the analyst what to look for. It does not tell them what they have. Validate intel-driven hypotheses against the actual telemetry before acting. The matched IOC is the question, not the answer.
</Callout>

<a id="enrichment"></a>
## Automated threat enrichment

Automated enrichment attaches threat-intelligence context to events as they arrive, so the analyst opens an alert that already carries reputation, attribution, and infrastructure detail instead of spending the first minutes of triage on manual lookups.

<div class="enrich-cols">
  <div class="enrich-col">
    <h3>Enrichment data types</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Reputation scores:</strong> trust ratings for IPs, domains, files, URLs.</li>
      <li><strong>Threat-actor attribution:</strong> adversary groups associated with observed indicators.</li>
      <li><strong>Malware classification:</strong> family, variant, and capability profiles.</li>
      <li><strong>Contextual metadata:</strong> domain registration data (RDAP, formerly WHOIS), infrastructure details, certificate validation.</li>
      <li><strong>Historical intelligence:</strong> previous indicator sightings and campaign activities.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="enrich-col">
    <h3>Common enrichment services</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>VirusTotal (Google):</strong> multi-engine file and URL reputation; the free community service remains, while enterprise access now runs through Google Threat Intelligence.</li>
      <li><strong>abuse.ch (URLhaus, ThreatFox, MalwareBazaar):</strong> free community tracking of malware URLs, IOCs, and samples.</li>
      <li><strong>urlscan.io:</strong> rendered-page snapshots and history for suspicious URLs.</li>
      <li><strong>Shodan / Censys:</strong> internet-exposed services and scan history.</li>
      <li><strong>GreyNoise:</strong> background-noise filtering and benign-activity identification.</li>
      <li><strong>VPN and proxy attribution (e.g., Spur, IPinfo):</strong> is this sign-in IP a commercial VPN, Tor, or a residential proxy?</li>
      <li><strong>Threat intelligence in Microsoft Defender (formerly Microsoft Defender Threat Intelligence / RiskIQ PassiveTotal):</strong> domain and IP infrastructure mapping. MDTI's convergence into the Defender portal and Microsoft Sentinel completed in August 2026, so it is no longer a standalone product.</li>
      <li><strong>LevelBlue Open Threat Exchange (OTX, formerly AlienVault OTX):</strong> crowd-sourced threat indicators and community intel.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="enrich-col">
    <h3>Implementation approaches</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Native tool integration:</strong> purpose-built connectors within SIEM/SOAR.</li>
      <li><strong>API orchestration:</strong> custom scripts or middleware for intelligence aggregation.</li>
      <li><strong>Intelligence platforms:</strong> dedicated TIP solutions with advanced enrichment.</li>
      <li><strong>Inline enrichment:</strong> network tools that tag traffic with real-time threat context.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout variant="warn" title="Enrichment without leaking">
Look up the hash before uploading a file. Anything submitted to a public multiscanner or public sandbox is retrievable by its subscribers, including the attacker watching for their own samples. Never upload documents that may contain internal or customer data; use a private sandbox. Prefer passive sources (passive DNS, scan history from Shodan, Censys or urlscan) to visiting or probing attacker infrastructure from corporate IP space, which can tip off the operator. Honor TLP markings: TLP:RED and TLP:AMBER+STRICT intel stays out of broadly visible tickets and third-party tools.
</Callout>

<a id="attribution"></a>
## Adversary attribution

Adversary attribution is the methodical process of connecting observed <DefineTerm term="Attack Patterns" display="attack patterns" /> to specific threat actors, groups, or campaigns. The analysis integrates behavioral signatures, <DefineTerm term="Infrastructure" display="infrastructure" /> reuse, <DefineTerm term="Malware" display="malware" /> family characteristics, and documented TTPs from vendor threat-intel reporting (Google Threat Intelligence Group, which absorbed Mandiant Intelligence; CrowdStrike; Microsoft Threat Intelligence; <DefineTerm term="Recorded Future" />), government advisories (CISA, NCSC), and MITRE ATT&amp;CK group pages, which cross-reference vendor reporting. Each vendor names and bounds clusters from its own telemetry, so no single source is authoritative on its own.

<div class="attribution-grid">
  <div class="attribution-card attribution-card--tech">
    <h3>Technical evidence</h3>
    <p>Malware code signatures, infrastructure overlap, compilation artifacts. Foundational linkages to known threat actors.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="attribution-card attribution-card--behav">
    <h3>Behavioral patterns</h3>
    <p>Victim selection criteria, operational timeframes, distinctive tactical approaches. Correlation points beyond the binary.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="attribution-card attribution-card--strat">
    <h3>Strategic context</h3>
    <p>Geopolitical developments, threat-actor motivations, industry targeting preferences. The "why" that anchors attribution.</p>
  </div>
</div>

### Confidence levels

Attribution claims use the same LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH scale as every other claim in the case file (the <a href="/docs/documentation/standards/#confidence">shared rubric</a>). The grid below translates each level into attribution-specific evidence, so the analyst knows what an attribution claim must rest on before it earns its label.

<div class="confidence-grid">
  <div class="confidence-card confidence-card--low">
    <span class="confidence-card__tag">LOW</span>
    <p>Isolated technical indicators with significant potential for coincidental overlap. May suggest investigative directions but requires substantial corroborating evidence before operational use.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="confidence-card confidence-card--med">
    <span class="confidence-card__tag">MEDIUM</span>
    <p>Multiple correlated indicators across diverse categories (tooling, infrastructure, TTPs) that collectively point toward a specific threat actor or group.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="confidence-card confidence-card--high">
    <span class="confidence-card__tag">HIGH</span>
    <p>Comprehensive evidence portfolio including unique identifiers, distinctive operational patterns, and multiple reinforcing data points indicating a specific actor with minimal alternative explanations.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<Reveal summary="Attribution challenges to recognize" variant="warn">

- **False flag operations:** sophisticated adversaries deliberately impersonate other threat actors to obscure responsibility and complicate attribution.
- **Shared tooling:** widespread use of common malware families or publicly available frameworks creates attribution ambiguity.
- **Name fragmentation:** each vendor names clusters from its own telemetry, and the boundaries differ. APT29 is Midnight Blizzard to Microsoft, Cozy Bear to CrowdStrike and, since July 2026, ICE RELIC to Google. Record the ATT&amp;CK group ID (G0016) next to whichever name the source used, and do not treat two vendors' names as proof of the same actor.
- **Evolution of TTPs:** actors change their techniques over time, so a profile built on last year's tradecraft can misattribute this year's intrusion.
- **Confirmation bias:** the analytical tendency to overvalue evidence that supports the first attribution hypothesis while discounting contradictory indicators.

Attribution at low or medium confidence should be labeled as such in the case file. Promoting low-confidence attribution into operational decisions is a common and costly attribution failure: the decision survives long after the caveat is forgotten.

</Reveal>

### Actor names across vendors

The same activity carries a different name in each vendor's reporting. Record the ATT&amp;CK group ID next to whichever name your source used, so two reports can be compared on one key.

| ATT&amp;CK group | Microsoft | CrowdStrike | Google (since July 2026) |
|---|---|---|---|
| APT29 (G0016) | Midnight Blizzard | Cozy Bear | ICE RELIC |
| APT28 (G0007) | Forest Blizzard | Fancy Bear | LAKE RELIC |
| Sandworm Team (G0034) | Seashell Blizzard | Voodoo Bear | SANDWORM RELIC (formerly APT44) |
| FIN7 (G0046) | Sangria Tempest | Carbon Spider | WILD COMET |

A crosswalk row means the vendors track overlapping activity, not that their clusters are identical: each vendor draws the boundary from its own telemetry. Treat a match across vendors' names as one more attribution input, not as confirmation.

## The program behind the intel

The rest of this page is the machinery that produces what reaches the triage desk: the indicator pipeline, TTP-based hunting, dark-web collection, and the intelligence tiers. Most of it is CTI-team and detection-engineering work. It is here so the analyst knows what the matches and labels they consume rest on.

## Indicator matching

Indicator matching compares local telemetry (DNS queries, process <DefineTerm term="Hash" display="hashes" />, outbound connections) against known IOCs from threat-intel sources. Sources span commercial threat feeds, government advisories, ISACs, open-source databases, and internal reports.

<div class="ioc-grid">
  <div class="ioc-card">
    <h3>Network indicators</h3>
    <p>IP addresses, URLs, and domain names associated with malicious infrastructure (C2 servers, phishing sites, malware staging).</p>
  </div>
  <div class="ioc-card">
    <h3>File indicators</h3>
    <p>Cryptographic hashes (MD5/SHA256) identifying known malware payloads or tools.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="ioc-card">
    <h3>Email indicators</h3>
    <p>Suspicious senders, subject patterns, attachment signatures from known campaigns.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="ioc-card">
    <h3>Host-based indicators</h3>
    <p>Artifacts known malware leaves on a host: registry keys and values, scheduled-task or service names, mutex names, dropped file paths. These are still exact-match indicators; matching on behavior is the TTP-based hunting below.</p>
  </div>
</div>

### Indicator matching process

The six steps below are the pipeline that stands up and maintains indicator matching, and most of it is CTI-team and detection-engineering work, not triage. The on-shift analyst works at steps 04 and 05 (querying newly published indicators against history and receiving real-time matches as alerts) and contributes to step 06 every time a closed case records whether a match was real. The rest is here so the analyst knows what the matches they consume rest on.

<div class="ti-process">
  <div class="ti-process__step">
    <span class="ti-process__num">01</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Ingestion</h4>
      <p>Collect and normalize indicators into structured formats (<DefineTerm term="Structured Threat Information Expression (STIX)" display="STIX" /> 2.1 objects over TAXII 2.1 feeds is the common standard; MISP and OpenCTI are widely used open-source platforms), preserving each item's source and TLP marking (FIRST TLP 2.0: RED, AMBER+STRICT, AMBER, GREEN, CLEAR).</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="ti-process__step">
    <span class="ti-process__num">02</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Validation</h4>
      <p>Evaluate indicator quality, relevance, and reliability before operational deployment.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="ti-process__step">
    <span class="ti-process__num">03</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Integration</h4>
      <p>Deploy matching capabilities across SIEM, EDR, NDR, and proxy systems.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="ti-process__step">
    <span class="ti-process__num">04</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Retroactive hunting</h4>
      <p>Analyze historical data to uncover previously undetected compromises using newly acquired indicators.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="ti-process__step">
    <span class="ti-process__num">05</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Real-time monitoring</h4>
      <p>Implement alerting mechanisms for immediate detection of indicator matches in ongoing activity.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="ti-process__step">
    <span class="ti-process__num">06</span>
    <div>
      <h4>Feedback</h4>
      <p>Systematically document true and false positives to continuously refine intelligence quality.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

Know the limits of what this pipeline delivers. Indicator matching is reactive and only as good as current, verified intelligence. Adversaries rotate domains and use <DefineTerm term="Fileless Malware" display="fileless malware" /> and living-off-the-land tradecraft precisely to slip past <DefineTerm term="Signature-Based Detection" display="signature-based detection" />. Matching is necessary, not sufficient: pair it with behavioral analysis and TTP-based hunting.

<a id="ttp-hunting"></a>
## Threat hunting with TTPs

Proactive <DefineTerm term="Threat Hunting" display="threat hunting" /> based on known Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures uncovers sophisticated threats that have evaded traditional detection. Unlike IOC matching, which relies on known <DefineTerm term="Artifacts" display="artifacts" />, TTP-based hunting focuses on adversary behavior patterns regardless of the particular tools employed.

By turning TTPs into testable hunt hypotheses (for example, "an adversary with a foothold is launching encoded <DefineTerm term="PowerShell" /> from Office applications: look for winword.exe or excel.exe spawning powershell.exe with -enc", or "an adversary is persisting with scheduled tasks whose action runs a binary from a user-writable temp path"), security teams can methodically query logs, EDR telemetry, or SIEM platforms for evidence of stealthy or emerging attacks. Note the shape: the hypothesis states what an adversary is doing and why it would show; the query is how you test it. Published hunting frameworks such as PEAK and TaHiTI formalize the same loop.

<div class="hunt-grid">
  <div class="hunt-card">
    <span class="hunt-card__num">01</span>
    <h3>Hypothesis formation</h3>
    <p>Develop specific, testable theories based on threat intelligence about adversary TTPs relevant to your environment.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="hunt-card">
    <span class="hunt-card__num">02</span>
    <h3>Query development</h3>
    <p>Create data queries or analytics that would identify evidence of the targeted behavior across relevant data sources.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="hunt-card">
    <span class="hunt-card__num">03</span>
    <h3>Hunt execution</h3>
    <p>Systematically search through current and historical data for patterns matching the hypothesis.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="hunt-card">
    <span class="hunt-card__num">04</span>
    <h3>Finding analysis</h3>
    <p>Investigate initial results to eliminate false positives and validate genuine security concerns.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="hunt-card">
    <span class="hunt-card__num">05</span>
    <h3>Process improvement</h3>
    <p>Document findings, refine queries, and implement persistent detection for discovered techniques.</p>
  </div>
</div>

### Why TTPs outlast indicators: the Pyramid of Pain

TTP-based hunting keeps working because behavior is expensive to change. David J. Bianco's Pyramid of Pain (2013) ranks indicators by how much it costs the adversary when defenders deny them: hash values (trivial), IP addresses, domain names, network and host artifacts, tools, and TTPs (tough). An attacker can recompile malware or stand up new infrastructure in an afternoon, but abandoning a technique that works means retooling an entire operation. The techniques stay stable long after every indicator has rotated.

<a id="dark-web"></a>
## Dark web and underground monitoring

Dark web surveillance can detect exposed credentials, breach preparations, targeted attack discussions, <DefineTerm term="Exploit" display="exploit" /> development, and brand impersonation. This is a program capability, not a triage activity: specialized vendors and dedicated threat-intel teams do the monitoring, using automated scraping plus human intelligence, and the collection challenges below are theirs to solve. What reaches the triage analyst is the output: a credential-exposure alert in the queue or an enrichment note on a case. Understanding the collection matters because it tells you how to weigh that output (where it came from, how noisy the space is, and why it arrives with a confidence label rather than as fact).

<div class="darkweb-grid">
  <div class="darkweb-card">
    <h3>Sources</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Criminal forums:</strong> boards for technique sharing, service sales, recruiting.</li>
      <li><strong>Marketplaces:</strong> underground markets for malware, exploits, credentials, stolen data.</li>
      <li><strong>Infostealer logs:</strong> passwords, session cookies and tokens harvested from infected devices, sold in markets and Telegram channels. Today's dominant source of credential exposure (see <DefineTerm term="Infostealers" display="infostealers" />).</li>
      <li><strong>Initial access brokers:</strong> listings that sell working access (VPN credentials, a session, a foothold) into named organizations.</li>
      <li><strong>Leak sites:</strong> where ransomware groups and hacktivists publish stolen data.</li>
      <li><strong>Chat platforms:</strong> encrypted messaging for private actor communications.</li>
      <li><strong>Paste sites:</strong> anonymous data dumps and credential leaks.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="darkweb-card">
    <h3>Intelligence value</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Pre-attack indicators before traditional detection fires.</li>
      <li>Visibility into adversary capabilities and intentions.</li>
      <li>Advance warning of emerging attack techniques.</li>
      <li>Insight into targeting priorities of threat actors.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="darkweb-card">
    <h3>Collection challenges</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Access barriers to closed communities.</li>
      <li>Language and cultural context requirements.</li>
      <li>Signal-to-noise ratio in unstructured data.</li>
      <li>Legal and ethical considerations in collection.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="darkweb-card">
    <h3>Use cases</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Credential exposure: reset the password, revoke active sessions and refresh tokens (a stolen session cookie can outlive a password reset), and review MFA methods, then find the infected device the log came from.</li>
      <li>Early vulnerability patching based on exploit chatter.</li>
      <li>Brand protection and impersonation detection.</li>
      <li>Spotting leaked internal data that points to an insider or a compromised account.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<Callout variant="info" title="When a sign-in has no phishing event behind it">
A sign-in or session with no phishing event behind it often traces to a stealer log from a personal or contractor device, so check stealer-log feeds for the user and the domain. Attacker traffic now routinely comes through residential proxies on consumer ISPs, so a clean IP reputation does not mean benign.
</Callout>

## Threat intelligence tiers

Intelligence operates across three tiers, each serving distinct purposes and distinct audiences. Triage runs almost entirely on the tactical tier; the operational tier serves threat hunters and detection engineers between cases; the strategic tier is program material for leadership and architects. The table is here so the analyst can place any piece of intel that crosses their desk, not because a triage case calls for a geopolitical briefing.

Some models split a fourth, technical tier for raw IOCs; here it is folded into tactical.

The tiers depend on each other. Tactical intel without operational context produces noise, and strategic intel without tactical depth produces strategy decks no one can operationalize. The methodology asks the SOC to use all three: tactical for triage, operational for hunting, strategic for the program-level decisions about what to detect and what to defend.

<KeyTakeaway>
Threat intelligence makes Uncover faster when used as a hypothesis generator. It makes Uncover slower when treated as ground truth. The methodology asks the analyst to use intel deliberately, score it across source/recency/specificity/corroboration, enrich without leaking, and confirm with the environment's own evidence before acting.
</KeyTakeaway>
